    Experiment 44: how much do labelled switching events hurt the best guess?

    Experiment 33 produced the best forecast it found of demand at the 16 Primary substations (substations that step power down to the local distribution network), which we call the best guess. We asked how much of the best guess's error, for July 2025 to June 2026, falls on half-hours affected by a switching event, an operation in which engineers moved load from one substation to a neighbouring substation. We split the summed error three ways: half-hours inside an event labelled in experiment 41, half-hours outside every event whose lagged power (the earlier power readings the forecast takes as inputs) falls inside one, and all other half-hours. We kept only the larger transfers that lasted over 24 hours, the events that experiment 41's labeller finds most reliably. The first two groups together are 1.35% of all half-hours scored and carry 3.4% of the summed error. A perfect switching detector would lower the summed error by 0.42%, with a 95% interval from -0.47% to 1.96%.

    Refuted: in the trustworthy band, a perfect detector would lower the quantile score by 0.42% (95% interval -0.47% to 1.96%) and the tail score by nothing, against a threshold of 3% set in the plan. The quantile score is twice the mean pinball loss over nine quantile levels, as in experiment 33, in points of capacity (1 point is 1% of a series' capacity). The tail score is the threshold-weighted continuous ranked probability score (twCRPS) at each series' 99th percentile of power, which counts only errors above that level. The trustworthy band is the visible events that moved at least 25% of the receiving substation's load and lasted over 24 hours; experiment 41's labeller recovered 83% of the artificial transfers of that size and length that it was tested on. The perfect-detector bound gives each flagged half-hour its series' mean score outside every flag, so the bound is the largest reduction a detector could deliver. The plan is in [PR

    1261](https://github.com/openclimatefix/nged-substation-forecast/pull/1261).

    The prediction held for the two ratios and failed for the size. Flagged half-hours are under 3% of all half-hours (1.35%), and their score share is 2.5 times their half-hour share, above the at-least-1.5 times the plan predicted. The flagged share did not reach the predicted 5% to 15%, and the bound did not reach the predicted 1% to 5%.

    The intervals are wide because only 6 trustworthy events fall in the scored year. The events lie on the series with time_series_id 10, 12, and 13, with onsets in July, September, and October 2025 and June 2026. The other 13 Primary series have no trustworthy event in the year, so the result says little about those 13 series. The intervals resample whole months of forecast origin, so they understate that uncertainty.

    What a detector could and could not help

    Only two groups of half-hours are open to a detector. They are the half-hours whose lagged power falls inside an event, and those whose target falls in an event that started before the forecast was made. The metered view scores the forecast against the power actually measured, which includes the effect of the switching, and counts both groups. The normal running arrangement (NRA) view scores against the power the electricity network would have carried without the switching, so it counts only the first group, scored outside every event. The ceiling also counts events that started after the forecast was made, which no detector can anticipate.

    For the quantile score, with the series-mean counterfactual, the bounds are:

    • Metered view: 0.42% (-0.47% to 1.96%).
    • NRA view: 0.34% (-0.51% to 1.79%).
    • Ceiling: 0.96% (-0.30% to 3.42%).

    The counterfactual that matches each flagged half-hour to unflagged half-hours of the same series, forecast lead time, and hour of day gives the same point estimates to two decimals, and interval ends within 0.02 points. For the tail score every bound is slightly negative (-0.85% for the metered view), because the flagged half-hours have a lower tail score than the unflagged half-hours.

    The all-visible sensitivity, which uses every event the labeller found visible whatever its size or duration, gives larger and far less certain figures. In the scored year it holds 22 events on 11 series. Flagged half-hours are 7.4% of the total (3.8% to 11.3%) and carry 11.6% of the quantile score (4.8% to 18.6%). The bounds are 2.35% (-0.22% to 5.73%) for the metered view and 4.11% (0.16% to 8.34%) for the ceiling.

    The all-visible tail results rest on a few short events. The flagged half-hours carry 55% of the tail score (2% to 70%), far more than their 11.6% of the quantile score, and the metered tail bound is 32.7% (-6.0% to 61.1%).

    Dropping the events that rest on one badly metered substation removes the effect. Of the 22 trustworthy events in all years, 10 have a receiving or donating substation with over half its readings missing during the event. Without them 2 trustworthy events remain in the scored year, and every bound's point estimate lies between -0.3% and -0.1%.

    Caveats

    • Few events. The decision rule was written in the plan before the scoring, and the verdict follows it. The month-resampling interval understates how little 6 events on 3 series can show.
    • The stored receiver shares are missing for most events. The labelled set's moved_share_of_receiver is NaN (missing) for 62 of the 81 visible events that touch a Primary series (82 in all). The causes are that the labeller wrote some donor or receiver ids as strings and the share code dropped them, and that 20 events name no metered receiver. This experiment recomputes the share as the moved MW over the named receivers' mean absolute power in the 28 days before the onset. The recomputed share agrees with the stored share where both exist (for example 0.33 against 0.34), but it ignores how the load splits between receivers, so it understates the share when a receiver takes only part of the load. The recomputed share exists for 61 events, of which 22 are 0.25 or more with a duration over 24 hours.
    • Slightly favourable detection time. An event counts as known to a detector once the forecast origin (the time the forecast is made) passes its onset, but telemetry arrives 3 hours late, so the score is slightly too favourable for events that start less than 3.5 hours before the origin.
    • Nested lag definitions. The correction's 14-day window (1.20% of half-hours) contains the three lags the forecast reads (0.48%), so the combined lagged-power group equals the window group.
    • Primary series only. The labels cover the 16 Primary series, so the bulk supply point and grid supply point series, solar farms, and wind farms have no figure.

    Lessons for later experiments

    • In this year, the labelled events do not justify a detector for the forecast error alone.
    • Figures 2 and 4 show that the all-visible tail-score share rests on a few short events, which a first detector experiment could look at before it looks at the whole year.
