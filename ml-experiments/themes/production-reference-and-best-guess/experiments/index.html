    Production reference and best guess: experiments

    This page lists every experiment tried in the theme, newest first. An experiment that belongs mainly to another theme but bears on this one is marked "also relevant".

    Experiment Hypothesis Verdict Production status Take-home message Relevance
    00333 primary-demand-foundation-model-zero-shot A pre-trained time-series foundation model (Chronos-2, 120 million parameters, Apache-2.0) forecasts a Primary substation's half-hourly demand from the substation's own history. Without weather inputs Chronos-2 cannot match Primary demand research default 3 at leads of 1 to 10 days. With the ensemble-mean temperature, the sun's elevation, and calendar columns passed as covariates, Chronos-2 closes part of the gap, but not all of it. partly supported not-for-production A pre-trained time-series foundation model, used without any training on our data, forecasts Primary demand clearly worse than Primary demand research default 3 at every lead from 0.5 to 240 hours. Giving the foundation model the temperature forecast, the sun's elevation, and the calendar recovers about one sixth of the gap, and makes sunny-daylight half-hours worse. Chronos-2, the one foundation model we tested, is not a candidate to replace the default, and a foundation-model forecast would have to be scored alone against the default before use as a blend member. primary
    00330 primary-demand-standard-architecture-arm The maintainer prefers one standard architecture (a physics core, an optional neural input adapter, an optional mixture-density output head) across demand, wind, and solar forecasts, unless a different architecture gains strongly. Primary demand has the physics core already (the convex stack with a Kalman filter). One small network, trained jointly to correct the median and to give a mixture-density band, fed through a learned two-layer adapter of the ensemble summaries, scores within 0.03 points of Primary demand research default 3, so demand can share the standard architecture at little or no loss. partly supported not-for-production A single neural network with a learned input adapter and a mixture-density output, placed on top of the frozen convex stack, forecasts Primary demand at least as well as the current research default from 0.5 to 168 hours, so demand does not need a different architecture from wind and solar at those leads. The gain comes from the mixture-density output (the median-only version gains nothing), and most of it comes from one Primary, D10. The 7-to-10-day range is untested, and the cold-night and sunny-daylight tails are too uncertain to call the architecture safe. primary
    00329 primary-demand-default-3-member-solar-distribution-at-7-to-10-days At 168 to 240 hours the 51 ensemble members have little skill for individual days, so the forecast centres on average sunshine and the half-hours that turn out sunny have realised demand below the median. The weakness of Primary demand research default 3 on sunny daylight (5.07 against the manual heuristic's 4.82 in experiment 317, for the convex chain before any blend) is therefore a fault of the predictive distribution (the lower tail on realised-sunny half-hours), not of the median. Replacing the two-number Gaussian summary of the members' solar responses with the 51 responses themselves (X1), mixing them with a climatology of the response (X2), or adding the members of the two previous daily runs (X3) fixes the distribution. not supported not-for-production The 7-to-10-day weakness on sunny half-hours no longer exists in the research demand forecast: the two-number solar summary and the daylight-only blend with the manual heuristic already took it from 5.2% worse than the heuristic to 6.6% better, so no new distribution method is needed there. What remains is one coastal Primary (D5), where the forecast is 11% worse than the heuristic, which points back at the solar term and not at the width of the bands. primary
    00325 primary-demand-default-3-sunshine-response-refit-and-restriction Experiment 323's sunshine response (F1) harmed all daylight half-hours because the Kalman filter's level, the winter night offset, and the median correction were estimated on the errors of a fit without a response and so counted the same error twice (double counting), or because the response acts too widely, at forecast clear-sky index where the damped ensemble-mean forecast index mis-applies the response. Refitting those corrections on the response's own errors (F1r) removes the first cause, and restricting the response to clear-sky index above 0.6 and sun elevation above 15 degrees (F1s) removes the second. small gain not-for-production The sunshine response, once the rest of the forecast chain is refitted around it, no longer makes daylight forecasts worse, but the response improves the overall forecast by under 0.01 points, too little to adopt. The response improves spring daylight at two Primaries (D5 and D9) by about 0.11 points. primary
    00323 primary-demand-default-3-flexible-sunshine-response-in-the-convex-fit Primary demand research default 3 trails the depth-1 XGBoost refit on April-to-June daylight half-hours because its solar term (the pinned capacity times the derated unit shape, with the D5 clip) is too high on clear spring middays. Experiment 322 grafted the refit's sunshine response onto the default and recovered 0.50 of the refit's advantage. A smooth correction in clear-sky index and sun elevation, fitted in the same bounded least-squares fit as the solar term, recovers a useful part of that gain with no model of other inputs. not supported not-for-production Adding a flexible sunshine curve to the solar part of the demand forecast does not recover the gap to the better-performing XGBoost model: it helps the sunniest half-hours but worsens the pooled score by about 0.4%. The advantage seen in experiment 322 is therefore not recovered by adding a smooth curve in clear-sky index and sun elevation on top of the existing level corrections, and most of the harm sits in the level shift. A boosted-tree sunshine term could use more inputs at once, and whether to test it is a decision for the research plan. primary
    00322 primary-demand-default-2-daylight-only-heuristic-blend-and-sunshine-graft-replication (Stages 1 and 2) In experiment 320 the heuristic blend's gain at 168 to 240 hours sits on sunny daylight (-0.236 points) and in April to October, while its harm sits on cold winter nights, which are night half-hours. Confining the blend of experiment 319 to half-hours with the sun above 5 degrees (D1) keeps the gain with no harm on cold winter nights by construction, and a night weight that is a function of the share of the 51 ensemble members below 3 degrees C (D3) recovers the nights where the heuristic helps without harming cold ones. (Stage 3, a diagnostic) Experiment 316's sunshine graft, which recovered 0.36 of the depth-1 XGBoost refit's advantage on the worst April-to-June half-hours, replicates in April and June 2025. partly supported candidate At 7 to 10 days the blend with the manual heuristic gains about 4% at all hours, and about 2.4% when it is used only by day, which is the version with no harm on the coldest winter nights. Counting how many of the 51 ensemble members are cold finds 72% of those nights (the ensemble mean finds 41%), and a hard switch on that count keeps the whole 4% with almost no cold-night harm, but that switch was not pre-registered. The sunshine response of the depth-1 XGBoost refit explains about half of the refit's advantage on the worst April-to-June half-hours in both years, so a flexible sunshine term in the convex fit is worth testing. primary
    00320 primary-demand-default-2-night-aware-heuristic-blend-and-night-band-width Primary demand research default 2 has two weak spots. (1) At 168 to 240 hours a blend with the manual heuristic lowers the score by 4.5%, but a single weight per lead band and season harms cold winter nights, where the convex chain is the better forecast. Default plus B1 (the blend switched off on half-hours forecast to be cold winter nights) or default plus B2 (a chain weight that is a logistic function of the ensemble-mean temperature, per lead band and day or night) keeps the gain without the harm. (2) The prediction bands are too wide at night, most clearly at one Primary (D10, whose band heads gained 0.144 points, 88% of it at night). Default plus N (a width multiplier on the quantile offsets from the median, fitted per Primary and per night or day block from the previous 90 days) removes that excess width at every Primary. partly supported candidate A blend with the manual heuristic still improves the 7 to 10 day Primary demand forecast by about 4%, and the harm it does on the coldest winter nights cannot be removed by switching it off when the weather forecast says the night will be cold, because at 7 to 10 days that forecast spots only between one and two cold nights in three. Narrowing the night prediction bands helps one Primary (D10, by 0.25 points on night half-hours), leaves the other five unchanged or slightly worse, and makes the cold-night tails worse. primary
    00319 primary-demand-default-1-solar-summary-and-heuristic-blend-at-7-to-10-days Primary demand research default 1 has two weak spots at 168 to 240 hours: sunny daylight half-hours, where it scores 5.2% worse than the manual heuristic, and April to October, where it beats climatology by only 4%. Default plus x1 (the two-number solar summary S, the mean and standard deviation of the 51 ensemble members' solar responses, extended from 0.5 to 168 hours out to 240 hours) lowers the sunny-daylight score at 168 to 240 hours. Default plus x2 (a blend of the convex chain's median with the manual heuristic's, with the weight fitted per 24-hour lead band and season group) lowers the pooled score at 168 to 240 hours, mostly in April to October. partly supported candidate The demand forecast for 7 to 10 days ahead improves by about 2% when its solar term also uses the spread between the 51 weather-forecast members, and the gain is largest on sunny days (6.6%), where the forecast had been worse than the manual heuristic that National Grid Electricity Distribution (NGED) staff use. Blending in the manual heuristic improves the forecast by 4.5%, but the blend worsens the coldest winter nights beyond the agreed tolerance, so only the solar change is adopted. primary
    00317 primary-demand-default-1-at-7-to-10-days The ensemble weather already reaches 10 days, so the convex model of Primary demand research default 1 can be driven to 240 hours, and with a spread table fitted per 24-hour lead band its quantile score stays better than the manual heuristic and climatology through day 9 and ties climatology on day 10. partly supported not-for-production The demand forecast we use for research still has a lower error than a table of typical demand by month and time of day (climatology) at 9 to 10 days ahead, by about 11%, because the temperature forecast keeps some skill there. Its advantage over NGED's manual heuristic shrinks to 5% to 9% beyond 7 days, and the 95% interval of that advantage includes zero. The advantage sits in winter and at two of the six substations. The forecast is useful out to 10 days on cold winter nights and no better than the heuristic on sunny daylight half-hours. primary
    00316 primary-demand-night-level-and-single-input-terms (Stage 0) The gain of the band heads of experiments 311 and 314 at D10, an anonymised Primary substation, is mostly a night-time level error of Primary demand research default 1 and not a better description of the uncertainty. (Stage 1) The April-to-June gap between arm D and BG2 at D5 and D9, two anonymised Primaries, sits mainly in arm D's response to sunshine and not in its recent-power or calendar terms. inconclusive not-for-production The band heads help at one Primary substation mainly by narrowing its night-time bands, not by correcting a level error: a night level offset reproduces only 14% of the gain, with a wide interval. For the April-to-June gap to BG2, a post hoc test points at the sunshine response of the best-guess model (0.36 of the gap recovered on the worst half-hours, but only 0.19 at one of the two Primaries), and a differently fitted surrogate in the audit did not reproduce it, so the ranking of the families is unreliable. primary
    00314 primary-demand-gated-or-pinball-band-head Experiment 311's mixture-density band head lowered the quantile score of Primary demand research default 1 but harmed sunny daylight half-hours, and its gain rested on D10. Keeping the default's bands on sunny daylight half-hours (gate), training the head directly on the 11-level pinball loss (quantile network), or both, removes the harm and leaves a gain that does not depend on one Primary. partly supported candidate Experiment 311's neural band head was stopped by harm on sunny daylight half-hours. Keeping the current bands on those half-hours fixes that harm, and the gated head scores 0.041 points better than the current Primary demand forecast bands (research default 1, lower is better). The gain is still almost entirely one substation, D10: without it the gain is 0.015 points and its interval touches zero, so the gated head is a candidate and not an adoption. Training the head on the quantile score directly gave no improvement. also relevant
    00311 primary-demand-mixture-density-band-head Primary demand research default 1 (the GAM stack A+H+S of experiments 220 and 251) gets its bands from a smooth spread table and a cold-day widening of the upper offsets, around a median shifted by the neural head. A mixture-density neural network, which reads the weather state and may be skewed, gives bands with a lower quantile score, and the gain sits in the protected tail groups. partly supported candidate A neural network that sets the forecast bands from the weather state scores 0.037 points better than the current Primary demand bands over the scored year, and 0.034 better in the year before, but the gain comes almost entirely from one substation (D10) and the new bands are worse on sunny daylight half-hours. The band head is a candidate, not an adoption; the next test should keep the current bands on sunny daylight half-hours or train the head on the quantile score directly. also relevant
    00310 metered-farm-long-lead-scoring Experiment 300's candidate (spread-binned plant, additive band, mixture head, blend with XGBoost) still beats XGBoost at every day band from 1 to 10 days ahead, and its margin over climatology shrinks as the lead grows. partly supported candidate The assembled solar-farm forecast still beats the seasonal-average forecast (climatology) at every lead up to 10 days, by 39% at day 1 and 10% at days 8 to 10, so NGED can use it across its whole horizon of 1 to 10 days ahead. A plant-fed XGBoost model loses its skill by day 8, so a simple first production forecast should be the mixture-density neural network on the plant, which is the best single family at every lead, and the blend of the GAM, MDN, and XGBoost adds more than 1% only at days 1 and 2. also relevant
    00306 summer-gap-solar-interactions-with-temperature-and-day-type The April-to-June daylight excess of arm D (the convex demand model with a Kalman filter on recent errors) over BG2 (the production-style XGBoost forecast) at the substations D5 and D9 is an additive-structure problem. On cold clear spring days heating demand and rooftop solar output are both large, and an additive model must give the solar term one weight on every day. Experiment 299 found the D5 excess largest on clear midday half-hours (+1.67 points). Letting the solar shape interact with temperature (shape times the heating hinge) and with day type (shape times a weekend indicator) lets the model give those days a different solar weight. refuted not-for-production Letting the rooftop-solar term vary with temperature and with weekdays versus weekends does not close the April-to-June gap to BG2: at the two substations with the gap, the extra columns close about 0% of it. Two linear interaction columns therefore do not explain the gap. The next test is what BG2 uses that the convex demand model does not (item 5 of the summer-gap brief). primary
    00305 summer-gap-what-bg2-knows-that-arm-d-does-not BG2 (experiment 87's production-style XGBoost forecast) beats the convex best-guess model (arm D) at the Primary substations D5 and D9 in April to June mainly through interactions of the sunshine inputs with temperature or with recent power, and not through the main effect of sunshine. refuted not-for-production The production-style forecast does not beat the convex best-guess model in April to June at two Primary substations through interactions of sunshine with temperature or with recent power: forbidding them removes under 3% of its advantage on the half-hours where the convex model trails most, and 16% on all daylight half-hours. A refit allowed no interactions at all keeps 92% of the advantage on the worst half-hours but only 45% on all daylight half-hours, so the worst half-hours come from the shape of single inputs and typical ones partly from other interactions. primary
    00300 metered-farm-best-guess-assembly The assembled candidate (spread-binned plant P2, a generalised additive model (GAM) band on the plant's residuals, a mixture-density head (MDN), blended per quantile with XGBoost) scores better than plant-fed XGBoost on the quantile score and at the 0.99 quantile, and its advantage is larger for a farm with no history because the plant carries what the missing history cannot. partly supported candidate The assembled solar-farm forecast (a physical power model, two correction stages, and a blend with XGBoost) scores 3.3% better than plant-fed XGBoost (XGBoost on the plant forecast, its ensemble spread, the unit mean, and the availability ratio, with no weather features of its own) on the quantile score, which averages the pinball loss over 11 quantile levels, when each farm trains on its own history. It is 43% better at the 0.99 quantile, which bounds how much output NGED may need to curtail. With no history for a farm, the gain over XGBoost is smaller (2.0%) and below the 3% we predicted, so the physical model helps less than we expected when a farm's history is missing. also relevant
    00299 summer-gap-timing-night-level-and-error-correction The April-to-June excess of arm D (the convex demand model with a Kalman filter on recent errors) over BG2 (the production-style XGBoost forecast) at the substations D5 and D9 is a step that starts at the March clock change and sits at short leads, which would point at a local-time mismatch or at BG2's use of recent power. D10's night-time level offset of about -2 points (21:00 to 07:30 UTC) is a step on one date, not a smooth drift with temperature. The Kalman filter and the correction head are net harmful at D5 and D9 in April to June. not supported not-for-production The spring excess of the best-guess demand forecast over BG2 grows with sunshine, appearing on clear midday half-hours at D5 and across the whole day at D9. It is not concentrated in the 4 weeks after the clock change or at short leads, so the next test is the solar term's response on clear half-hours at D5. The Kalman filter and the correction heads are not the cause: removing the filter changes little, and at D5 the heads reduce the excess. D10's night-time offset varies partly with month and has no single date on which it changes. primary
    00289 summer-gap-weather-input-discriminator The best-guess stack's weaker April to June score than BG2 at the decision Primaries comes from the ensemble's sunshine forecast (or its ratio calibration), and not from demand absorbing the solar term or from the error-correction layers. partly supported not-for-production Giving the best-guess stack the sunshine that actually happened would close about 70% of its April to June gap to BG2, but the same change helps the stack nearly as much in July to September, when the stack already matches BG2. Most of the April to June gap beyond the July to September gap therefore stays. That points to the solar term in the demand model, or to the error-correction layers, and away from the ensemble sunshine forecast. A refit with realised sunshine as the solar driver, and a check of the solar term at D5 and D9 in April to June, are the next steps. primary
    00281 economy7-night-step-smooth-in-temperature A forecast of primary substation demand whose night level is a set of free levels for each UTC night half-hour (experiment 121's NS-U), and then a step whose size is a monotone function of the cold, scores better in the Economy 7 step hours and on cold winter nights than the same forecast with only the winter night offset (experiment 155), when the weather input is the ensemble-mean forecast and not past weather. Economy 7 is the tariff that switches storage heaters on shortly after 00:30 UTC. Experiment 121 found a gain of 0.233 points on winter nights on past weather and asked for a test on weather forecasts. inconclusive not-for-production Fitting the Economy 7 night levels inside the demand model, and not adding a winter night offset afterwards, lowered the day-ahead error by about 0.02 to 0.03 points of rated demand over all rows in both years and by about 0.04 points on winter nights, but the gain in the two hours after the step starts could not be shown: it changes sign between the two years and depends on two or three substations. Letting the step grow with the cold added almost nothing over free night levels. The Economy 7 term does not explain the night-time gap at D10 in April to June. also relevant
    00280 pv-empirical-shape-and-capacity-in-arm-d The April to June 2026 gap between arm D (the monthly-refit generalised additive model of experiments 155 and 220, scored on experiment 178's rows) and BG2 comes from its solar term: the pinned solar capacity is too high and the south-facing fleet shape is not the shape the metered farms show. Replacing the fleet shape by the pooled empirical shape of experiment 269, at the capacity that shape's real-series fit supports, lowers arm D's April to June error at the substations where the pinned capacity looks too high (D5, D9, and D16). not supported not-for-production Putting the empirical solar shape into the forecast, at the pinned capacity or at the lower capacity the real series supports, does not lower the April to June error at the substations where the pinned capacity looked too high. The change is under 0.01 MW against an error of about 0.5 MW, and the intervals are wide. A capacity cut that is correct for the whole of April to June is not the fix either: at D5 it raises the error. This experiment finds no sign that the shape or the capacity of the solar term closes the summer gap to BG2. also relevant
    00279 dp-heating-curve-monotone-lagged-temperature-and-wind The heating response of the convex demand model T-all-K (a hinge at 15.5 C on four trailing-mean temperatures, split by day type) is too rigid. A smooth, monotone, non-negative curve on the same temperatures (A1), the same curve also on exponentially weighted and previous-day mean temperatures that carry the building's thermal lag (A2), or a small monotone neural network on three daily mean temperatures and the wind speed (A3), lowers the day-ahead quantile score on cold nights and winter days at the five decision Primaries, and the fitted curve is not a straight line. not supported not-for-production A smooth heating curve, a thermal lag, and wind each leave the day-ahead forecast within about 0.02 points of the straight-line heating response, so the evidence does not justify adding any of them. A small neural network learns a plausible curved heating response and lowers the error on cold nights by 0.13 points, but that gain comes from one cold spell in January 2026, did not appear in the previous year, and the network failed a test on synthetic data, so the substation card keeps the straight line for now. also relevant
    00278 stack-retraining-cadence The best-guess stack (arm D, the neural network head, and the two-number solar summary) needs its neural network head retrained at least quarterly, but its convex model (a generalised additive model with a Kalman filter on its coefficients) tolerates quarterly instead of monthly refits, because the filter absorbs the drift between refits. not supported not-for-production Retraining the small neural network that corrects the demand forecast's median, every quarter or every month, scored no better than training it once before the scored year, so it needs no retraining schedule over a year. The convex demand model should keep its monthly refits, which take 0.4 s per substation: refitting quarterly instead scored 0.010 points worse overall (not shown to be acceptable) and weekly refits gained nothing. Refitting the two solar factors quarterly instead of monthly changed nothing. primary
    00277 lsc-curtailment-metric-on-existing-forecasts The curtailment-cost metric of experiment 268 (studies.node_curtailment, sorted quantiles) ranks the four existing forecasts as the other metrics do, and can resolve the differences between them well enough to judge the load-shift questions of the decision program. supported not-for-production Over a year of ordinary days, the curtailment metric ranks the four existing forecasts the way the other scores do, and it can tell the GAM stack from BG2 (BG2 costs GBP 7.35 million more on the six Primaries at the 0.5 limit, a figure that ranks forecasts and is not money saved). Inside the windows around the 7 logged load transfers, the GAM stack cannot be separated from BG2, because those windows hold only 926 to 2,923 scored half-hours. Questions about load shifts need injected worlds with many more shifts, not the real record. also relevant
    00266 summer-gap-diagnostics-solar-term The best-guess demand stack (experiment 251's arm 1) trails BG2, the production-style XGBoost forecast, in April to June 2026 mainly because of its explicit solar term (the subtracted estimate of unmetered rooftop solar output), and not because of the neural-network head, the two-number summary of the ensemble's solar response, the Kalman filter, or the heating and lighting terms. The same defect explains the loss at D5, an anonymised Primary substation, on sunny half-hours. partly supported not-for-production The best-guess stack trails BG2 in April to June because of the solar term at two of the six substations (D5 and D9 on daylight half-hours) and a night-time error at a third (D10), not because of the neural-network head, the two-number summary, the Kalman filter, or the ensemble sunshine forecast; the head and summary actually reduce the gap.
