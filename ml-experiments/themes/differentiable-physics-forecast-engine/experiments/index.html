    Differentiable physics: the forecast engine: experiments

    This page lists every experiment tried in the theme, newest first. An experiment that belongs mainly to another theme but bears on this one is marked "also relevant".

    Experiment Hypothesis Verdict Production status Take-home message Relevance
    00306 summer-gap-solar-interactions-with-temperature-and-day-type The April-to-June daylight excess of arm D (the convex demand model with a Kalman filter on recent errors) over BG2 (the production-style XGBoost forecast) at the substations D5 and D9 is an additive-structure problem. On cold clear spring days heating demand and rooftop solar output are both large, and an additive model must give the solar term one weight on every day. Experiment 299 found the D5 excess largest on clear midday half-hours (+1.67 points). Letting the solar shape interact with temperature (shape times the heating hinge) and with day type (shape times a weekend indicator) lets the model give those days a different solar weight. refuted not-for-production Letting the rooftop-solar term vary with temperature and with weekdays versus weekends does not close the April-to-June gap to BG2: at the two substations with the gap, the extra columns close about 0% of it. Two linear interaction columns therefore do not explain the gap. The next test is what BG2 uses that the convex demand model does not (item 5 of the summer-gap brief). also relevant
    00305 summer-gap-what-bg2-knows-that-arm-d-does-not BG2 (experiment 87's production-style XGBoost forecast) beats the convex best-guess model (arm D) at the Primary substations D5 and D9 in April to June mainly through interactions of the sunshine inputs with temperature or with recent power, and not through the main effect of sunshine. refuted not-for-production The production-style forecast does not beat the convex best-guess model in April to June at two Primary substations through interactions of sunshine with temperature or with recent power: forbidding them removes under 3% of its advantage on the half-hours where the convex model trails most, and 16% on all daylight half-hours. A refit allowed no interactions at all keeps 92% of the advantage on the worst half-hours but only 45% on all daylight half-hours, so the worst half-hours come from the shape of single inputs and typical ones partly from other interactions. also relevant
    00299 summer-gap-timing-night-level-and-error-correction The April-to-June excess of arm D (the convex demand model with a Kalman filter on recent errors) over BG2 (the production-style XGBoost forecast) at the substations D5 and D9 is a step that starts at the March clock change and sits at short leads, which would point at a local-time mismatch or at BG2's use of recent power. D10's night-time level offset of about -2 points (21:00 to 07:30 UTC) is a step on one date, not a smooth drift with temperature. The Kalman filter and the correction head are net harmful at D5 and D9 in April to June. not supported not-for-production The spring excess of the best-guess demand forecast over BG2 grows with sunshine, appearing on clear midday half-hours at D5 and across the whole day at D9. It is not concentrated in the 4 weeks after the clock change or at short leads, so the next test is the solar term's response on clear half-hours at D5. The Kalman filter and the correction heads are not the cause: removing the filter changes little, and at D5 the heads reduce the excess. D10's night-time offset varies partly with month and has no single date on which it changes. also relevant
    00266 summer-gap-diagnostics-solar-term The best-guess demand stack (experiment 251's arm 1) trails BG2, the production-style XGBoost forecast, in April to June 2026 mainly because of its explicit solar term (the subtracted estimate of unmetered rooftop solar output), and not because of the neural-network head, the two-number summary of the ensemble's solar response, the Kalman filter, or the heating and lighting terms. The same defect explains the loss at D5, an anonymised Primary substation, on sunny half-hours. partly supported not-for-production The best-guess stack trails BG2 in April to June because of the solar term at two of the six substations (D5 and D9 on daylight half-hours) and a night-time error at a third (D10), not because of the neural-network head, the two-number summary, the Kalman filter, or the ensemble sunshine forecast; the head and summary actually reduce the gap. The pinned solar capacity looks too high in those months, not too low, and clear mornings are forecast worse than clear afternoons. Candidate next experiments are the export clip at D9, a morning-and-afternoon solar shape, and a look at D10's night residuals; the stack's added parts need no change for this gap. also relevant
    00251 dp-final-best-guess-assembly-clip-and-coast-against-bg2 The full best-guess stack of experiment 220 (arm D, the neural-network head, and the two-number solar summary) beats BG2, the manual heuristic, climatology, and a weak production-style XGBoost model over the scored year of 2025-07 to 2026-06. Adding the hand-set 60% clip at D5 lowers the D5 error on sunny half-hours when the neural-network head is absent, and adds nothing measurable when the head is present, because experiment 232 found the head cancels most of the clip's gain. supported candidate Over the one year we can score, the full best-guess demand forecast (arm D, a small neural network that corrects its median, and a two-number summary of the ensemble's solar response) beats BG2 by 0.136 points of quantile score (5%; lower is better) and the manual heuristic by 0.818 points, and it beats the manual heuristic on every headline metric we scored, including curtailment cost. The gain over BG2 is a winter gain: the stack is level with BG2 in summer and worse in April to June, and at D5 on sunny hours it is clearly worse than BG2. Adding the 60% solar clip at D5 helps only when the neural-network head is left out at D5, so the next work is the solar term at D5, not another stacked part. also relevant
    00244 dp-deep-ensemble-heads-cold-night-bands On cold nights the demand forecast's error is partly something the correction network does not know (epistemic uncertainty, as opposed to the weather forecast's own uncertainty, which experiment 192 found the 51 ensemble members' temperatures do not signal). The disagreement between 20 independently trained copies of the pooled correction network therefore predicts the absolute cold-night error, and widening the bands by a non-decreasing function of that disagreement lifts cold-night q95 coverage to 0.90. inconclusive not-for-production The disagreement between 20 independently trained correction networks does not tell us how wrong the forecast will be on cold nights (correlation 0.00), so it cannot set the width of the cold-night bands: scaling the bands by it lifts the 95% quantile's coverage by 0.006, the same as a shuffled spread does. The check that the spread reacts to an unusual input failed for a realistic 4 C error, so the experiment cannot say more. The cold-night shortfall is therefore unlikely to be fixed by training more networks; the experiment cannot say what would fix it. primary
    00234 no-xgboost-forecast-audit-part-1 For Primary substation demand, a metered solar farm, and a metered wind farm, the best forecast that uses no XGBoost anywhere scores within 2% of the quantile score of the best forecast that uses XGBoost, at 12 to 36 hour leads. partly supported candidate An XGBoost-free forecast of Primary substation demand scores 7.5% better than BG2, the XGBoost best guess, at 12 to 36 hours ahead, on one year and six substations. The XGBoost-free solar and wind forecasts score 2.8% and 5.0% worse, and lose at the 0.01, 0.05, and 0.95 quantiles. Only the solar plant model's 0.99 quantile beats XGBoost, and that is the tail that matters for curtailment. The next experiment tests small XGBoost components inside the XGBoost-free solar and wind forecasts. also relevant
    00228 dp-bg-rolling-origin-stack-against-bg2 The best-guess stack (arm D with experiment 169's bands, plus the network head of experiment 201) beats BG2 in most monthly origins and at most substations, and its advantage is larger in winter than in summer. partly supported candidate Over the one year we can score, the full best-guess stack (arm D, the neural network head, and the solar summary) beats BG2 by 0.136 points of quantile score (5% of BG2's 2.715; lower is better), and most of the gain comes from November to March. From April to June 2026 the stack is worse than BG2, and arm D without the neural network head is worse than BG2 in summer. The year-long gain is only just separable from zero, and we cannot test other years until BG2 is re-run for them. also relevant
    00225 dp-network-heads-on-unlabelled-worst-days Experiments 172, 174, and 175 found that the worst 5% of substation-days carry 41.7% of the excess loss of arm D (the best-guess convex forecast of experiment 155), and that 37 of the 72 worst days at the five decision substations carry none of the labels (switching event, holiday, cold snap, flagged reading, Economy 7 step). Experiments 200 and 201 found that a neural network corrects arm D mainly on very hot and very cold days. The unlabelled worst days therefore sit at temperature extremes, and the neural network cuts their loss. partly supported not-for-production The neural network does cut the loss on the 37 worst days that no label explains, by about a fifth, and cuts it by more than on the 35 days that do carry a label. The unlabelled worst days are not at temperature extremes, so a neural network aimed at the hottest and coldest days would not target them. What helps on them is the forecast input: a neural network without one removes 4%. primary
    00222 dp-network-before-filter-order Experiments 200 and 201 correct arm D after the Kalman filter. Their gain is mostly a systematic error of the convex model at some temperatures and loads, not a recent-error effect, and that systematic error currently sits in the filter's level state. A network that reads only the convex model's own output and inputs, and corrects the convex model before the filter, leaves the filter a cleaner residual stream and so beats the same kind of network applied after the filter. inconclusive not-for-production The gain of the network after the filter does not come from the convex model's own systematic error as seen in its past residuals. A network trained on those residuals learned patterns that do not carry to the next year, and when it was stopped on a later year it learned nothing and left the score unchanged. The test could not settle the order question, because the network failed to recover a known injected error. primary
    00220 dp-bg-stage4-assembly-with-drop-one The three parts added to the best-guess demand forecast since experiment 170 remove mostly different errors, so their gains add. The parts are the two-number solar summary of the 51 ensemble members (experiments 193 and 202), the pooled correction network on arm D's mean (experiments 200, 201, and 209), and the coast-and-widen rule for missing readings (experiment 205). The solar summary changes only daylight bands, and the network corrects arm D's median from its own output, the calendar, and temperature, so the two act on different rows and different error. The network may remove what the winter night offset and recipe R removed (both correct arm D's mean by time of day), and it may make the bands' cold-night widening unnecessary. partly supported candidate Adding two parts to the best-guess demand forecast lowers its quantile score by 0.136 points (5%) of substation effective capacity. The parts are a small neural network that corrects the forecast's median, and a two-number summary of the 51 ensemble members' solar response. Each part's gain is almost exactly what it achieves alone, so the two parts remove different errors. The network's own 11 quantiles reached 0.904 coverage of the 95% quantile on cold nights without the hand-fitted band fixes, and scored 0.048 points better than the corrected forecast overall, mostly at one substation. The coast-and-widen rule for missing readings was not scored with the stack. primary
    00216 dp-network-shape-on-arm-d-flexible-temperature-tails The neural network of experiments 200 and 201 lowers the quantile score of arm D (the best-guess convex forecast of experiment 155) by 0.09 to 0.125 points, mainly by raising the median on very hot and very cold days. That correction is a shape the convex model could carry. The convex model's only temperature terms are one-sided heating hinges at 15.5 degrees Celsius, so it has no term for cooling load and no extra slope in the cold tail. Extra hinge columns in the tails of the site's own temperature range, with a weaker ridge penalty on those columns, recover at least half of the network's gain without a network. inconclusive not-for-production Adding extra temperature terms for the hottest and coldest days to the convex demand model recovers only a fifth to a quarter of the neural network's improvement, and the recovery was not confirmed in a second year. The neural network's correction is spread over all temperatures instead of sitting at the extremes, and a smooth function of temperature explains under 1% of it, so the neural network uses an input other than temperature. The formal verdict is capped at inconclusive because a placebo check narrowly failed, but the shortfall does not depend on that check. primary
    00211 dp-boosted-tree-calibrator-and-input-ablations The 0.065 points by which the network without error inputs (experiment 200) beats the best lookup table (experiment 209) comes from a smooth or higher-order dependence on a few inputs, so a gradient-boosted tree model fitted with the pinball loss on the same inputs captures it, and a boosted-tree calibrator can replace the neural network. One-at-a-time removal of the network's inputs shows which input carries the gain. supported candidate A gradient-boosted tree model given the same inputs as the neural network, and no recent errors, lowers the error score of our best day-ahead forecast by about as much as the network does (0.098 against 0.100 points). The interval is wide, so a loss of up to 0.03 points against the network is not ruled out. The gain comes mostly from two inputs, the forecast level and the weather-data era. primary
    00209 dp-pinball-table-vs-network-head-on-arm-d The gain that experiment 200's network head and its blind copy get over arm D (0.100 to 0.125 points) is a recalibration that a table without hidden layers can reproduce. The table gives arm D's median a shift that depends on site, half-hour, day of week, lead band, and arm D's forecast level by an ensemble-era flag, and scales arm D's quantile offsets by site, lead band, and six-hour block. It is fitted by gradient descent on the pinball loss over the 11 quantiles, on the same rows as the network. A second arm adds a temperature-extremes term (a median lift below -3 degrees Celsius and above 24 degrees Celsius, from experiment 201's finding). not supported not-for-production A lookup table of corrections to our best day-ahead forecast lowers its error score by only 0.03 to 0.04 points, against 0.10 points for a neural network given similar inputs and no recent errors. This table design therefore does not match the network, and the cause is not yet isolated. A temperature-extremes term lowers the score by under 0.001 points. primary
    00205 dp-gamk-gap-robustness-coast-or-frozen-gam When power readings go missing, a Kalman filter that coasts (keeps running with no update, so the transient decays and the level holds) beats the frozen convex model (GAM) alone for short gaps and loses to it for long gaps, so a crossover gap length exists between 6 and 72 hours. The mechanism is that the level is a random walk, so a short gap leaves it nearly right, while a long gap lets it drift away from the demand. Widening the quantile bands by the filter's own growth in predictive variance keeps the 5% and 95% quantiles calibrated at the gap lengths where coasting is still better than the frozen model. inconclusive candidate When readings stop for 3, 6, 24, or 72 hours, keep running the Kalman filter, which corrects the convex demand model using its recent forecast errors, without feeding it new readings, and widen the quantile bands. At each of those four gap lengths that forecast scored 0.25 to 0.44 points of quantile score better than the convex demand model with no correction (a quantile score is an error measure in percent of the substation's effective capacity, and lower is better; the gain is 8% to 14% of the uncorrected model's score). At 72 hours the 99.4% bootstrap interval for the gain runs from 0.04 to 0.48 points, so the gain may be small. The data cannot show whether dropping only the filter beats coasting after a gap of about 72 hours: at every gap length the difference between the two forecasts is smaller than the variation between months. The forecast with the filter, winter night offset, and recipe R was still 0.025 points worse than its no-gap score 48 hours after a 24-hour gap ended. The filter alone was within 0.02 points of its no-gap score 24 hours after the gap ended. primary
    00202 dp-ens-two-number-rolling-replay Experiment 193 found that two numbers per half-hour (the mean and the standard deviation of the 51 ensemble members' solar responses) lower the daylight quantile score by 2.20% in the scored year. Its two fitted factors per lead band were fitted on interleaved months (the odd months of 2024-07 to 2025-06), some of them later than the replicate months they were applied to, and its numbers were held in float64. A replay of the live weather layer, which stores the two numbers as float32 columns computed from the one ensemble run published before each origin and refits the factors each month on history that has completed before that month, keeps most of the gain. supported candidate A weather layer that stores two numbers per half-hour (the mean and spread of the 51 ensemble members' solar-generation estimates) and refits its two scaling factors every month, using only runs that had finished before the month began, lowers the daylight quantile score (the score for the forecast's uncertainty bands) by 2.3% at five substations over one year, which is as much as running all 51 members. This confirms experiment 193's result under production-style timing, so the next step is to build the two columns into the weather layer. primary
    00201 dp-gam-residual-mlp A small neural network that reads the convex model's own design features, and later the calendar, the ensemble-mean weather, and lagged power, can learn an interaction the additive convex model lacks, and so lowers the quantile score of the best-guess convex forecast (arm D of experiment 155) when it adds a correction to the median and rescales the band widths. partly supported not-for-production A small neural network that reads the convex demand model's forecast lowers that forecast's quantile score by about 0.09 points (3%), and adding weather, calendar, or recent power does not help further. The neural network corrects an under-forecast on very hot and very cold days. That points to a missing temperature term in the convex demand model. The correction also scores worse on bank holidays and, in point estimate, on Christmas. primary
    00200 dp-pooled-correction-head-on-kalman-state Experiment 142's small network failed its known-answer gate with about 1,100 samples from one year at five substations. The same kind of network, trained on rows pooled across many Primary substations and years, learns to correct arm D's forecast from the recent errors, the calendar, the lead, and the ensemble-mean weather, and so beats recipe R (subtract 0.75 times the mean error of the last 14 days) at day D+1. partly supported not-for-production A small neural network trained on arm D's own past errors lowers the day-ahead score by 0.07 to 0.12 points at five substations, but a copy that sees no recent error gets most of that gain. The gain comes from correcting how arm D's error depends on its forecast level and the calendar, and a pinball-loss table comes within 0.01 to 0.02 points of the network on one year of data. primary
    00197 dp-kalman-level-adaptation-retest Experiment 190's known-answer gate could not be passed by a filter with one state per hour band, because the target's band is usually unread since an offset began. Scored only on pairs the filter can know about, (a) a faster per-band filter (A1) recovers at least 50% of a 0.10-size, 16-hour offset on target bands already read, and (b) a filter whose bands share one level while a same-sign run lasts (A3) recovers at least 50% on target bands not yet read, because it moves bands that no reading has reached. not supported not-for-production Making the Kalman filter's level state faster does not fix offsets that last for hours. Faster settings recover a long offset only late, and only by scoring worse on quiet days, and a level shared across the four hour bands recovered under a fifth of the offset, because its run counter cannot tell an offset from noise. No setting on the grid tried recovered an injected offset without scoring worse on quiet days, so level adaptation is dropped. primary
    00193 dp-ens-two-number-solar-summary Experiment 187 found that passing each of the 51 ensemble members through the solar term lowers the daylight quantile score of the full best-guess stack by 2.23%, and that a Gaussian with each row's own mean and spread of the 51 responses recovers 95% of that gain. Two numbers per half-hour (the mean and the standard deviation of the members' solar responses) therefore carry nearly all of the gain, so a serving path can store two columns instead of running 51 passes. supported candidate Summarising the 51 ensemble weather members' solar estimates by their mean and standard deviation lowers the daylight quantile score by about 2% (98% of the gain from running all 51 members, 93% in the replicate months), at the five substations scored over one year. The weather layer could store two columns per half-hour instead of 51, and the 10th and 90th percentiles add nothing measurable. A follow-up experiment could test the two numbers in the live weather layer. primary
    00192 dp-ens-members-temperature-cold-nights Experiment 187 found that passing the 51 ECMWF ensemble members through the solar term of the demand model lowers the daylight quantile score by 2.2%. The demand response to heating is also nonlinear (a hinge at 15.5 degrees C), and the ensemble mean smooths away the cold tail. Passing each member's temperature through the temperature terms therefore lowers the quantile score on cold winter nights. not supported not-for-production Running each ensemble member's temperature through the demand model gave no detectable improvement in the forecast bands on cold winter nights, because the 51 members' temperatures differ too little to signal the day's error. The cold-night bands are already too narrow. The cause of the cold-night error is still unknown; widening the bands directly, and testing wind, night cloud cover, and switching as inputs, are the candidates. primary
    00191 dp-price-sensitive-assets-in-the-history Some of D8's multi-day drops (experiment 159) and the matching shortfalls at its neighbour D14 are output of price-following gas generation (experiment 186), which no weather input explains and no forecast of the price can reach at a lead of days. Letting the convex demand model (GAM-K) see the half-hourly price in its training history, gated by the registered connection date of the 6 MW gas engine, lets it learn a cleaner baseline for the rest of the demand. The forecast then improves even when the price is unknown at forecast time. not supported not-for-production Modelling a price-following asset in the history does not make the forecast better at D8 or D14. A demand model that is given the true price at forecast time, which no live service has here, gains only 0.6% at D8, and the models that use a price available at forecast time lose 0.9% to 4.4%. The gas engine's response is real enough to fit (1.2 to 1.9 MW at D8 within the training rows), so the remaining D8 error comes from causes that these price indicators do not explain, and NGED's half-hourly output for the engine is the data to ask for next. also relevant
    00190 dp-kalman-level-adaptation-gated-boost The Kalman filter of the convex demand model (GAM-K) has a level state that is a random walk with one shared process-noise setting. A single setting must trade speed on a real level shift against noise on ordinary days. A gate that multiplies the level's process noise by m only while the last k one-step innovations (pooled over the four hour bands) share one sign and their mean exceeds z times the day-before innovation spread gives speed when a shift looks real and keeps the quiet setting otherwise. The mechanism predicts that the gate lowers the excess loss on days where the residual keeps one sign for hours (the 15 smooth-offset days of experiment 175), without harming ordinary days. inconclusive not-for-production A Kalman filter whose level state speeds up when the residual keeps one sign scored the same as the current filter (+0.0002 points), and an always-fast version scored 0.023 points worse. The test cannot say that filter speed is useless: its known-answer gate could not have been passed by any filter with one state per hour band, and a faster filter did recover offsets where they were visible. A fair test needs a redesigned gate and a wider grid of multipliers. primary
    00187 dp-ens-members-in-stack Experiment 184 found that running each of the 51 ensemble members through the solar term of the convex demand model lowers the daylight quantile score by 2.2%, but its verdict was capped at inconclusive because a monotone gate failed on rows where the unit PV shape itself falls as irradiance rises. With a gate that matches that shape, the same arm passes, and its gain lies where the best-guess stack leaves the largest sunny-daylight error, at the substation D5. supported candidate Running each of the 51 ensemble weather members through the solar part of the demand model still improves the daylight forecast bands by about 2% on top of the full best-guess stack, and night is untouched. About two thirds of the gain needs that day's own members, and two numbers per half-hour (the mean and spread of the members' solar responses) carry nearly all of it, so a cheaper two-number version is the next thing to test. primary
    00185 dp-price-dfs-national-signals-past-demand A national signal that experiment 177 found on many of the 37 unlabelled worst days (a high national system price, or a Demand Flexibility Service (DFS) event) explains part of the day-to-day variation in the best-guess forecast's loss over all days of the scored year, not only on the 37 worst days.
