    CAL: tail calibration and a coherence pass

    The maintainer postponed CAL on 2026-10-20. The question is whether the tails of the convex model with the Kalman filter (GAM-K) on ECMWF ensemble weather are calibrated in every lead band, and which re-spreading of the same mean forecast fixes the bands that are not. Experiments 107 and 111 found the pooled tails close to the nominal 95% and 99%, so the work is in particular cells: some substations in winter, the night hours, and one or two lead bands. Curtailment depends on the upper tail, so those cells matter more than the pooled figure.

    The mean forecast stays fixed, and only the spread changes. Four methods are candidates, each reusing the stored fits of experiment 111 so that no new model is fitted:

    • a rolling conformal offset per substation, lead band, and quantile;
    • the Kalman filter's own predictive variance, scaled by a table of standardised residuals;
    • the same variance widened by the spread across the ensemble members;
    • separate lower and upper widening factors per lead band.

    A cell counts as calibrated when its coverage lies within a binomial band set by the number of distinct substation-days in the cell. A substation-day is the unit because the errors of one substation's half-hours within a day move together. Before any method is scored, a blocking known-answer test checks that the band does not flag a forecast that is calibrated by construction, and that the band and the methods detect and remove a miscalibration of the size that matters. The decision rule asks for a method that passes in the replicate months and in the scored year without raising the quantile score by more than 0.02 points of effective capacity.

    The coherence pass tests whether the ensemble sums across substations are calibrated against the observed sum. Calibrated marginals for each substation do not make the sum of several substations calibrated, because the sum depends on how the errors of different substations move together. The test turns each substation's calibrated quantiles back into ensemble members with ensemble copula coupling (ECC), or with the Schaake shuffle, which orders the members by a template taken from history. It then sums the members across substations and scores the summed ensemble against the observed sum, and scores the joint distribution with the variogram score. The staged probabilistic forecast already specifies ECC and the coherence scores for the production forecast. The pass asks whether the convex model's spread, once calibrated, keeps the cross-substation dependence that those scores reward. It runs after the per-substation calibration, on the same rows.

    N7: paired residuals at switching events

    Where both sides of a hand-filtered switching event are metered, the donor's and the receiver's residuals should be anti-correlated and sum to roughly the moved power. The experiment reports the share of events where the two residuals sum to within 20% of the moved power. A high share would show that a later model can treat a transfer as a conserved flow between two nodes. Only a minority of the events have both sides metered, so the result describes those events and does not generalise to the rest. The run takes minutes on the CPU.

    Schaake shuffle against ECC

    The comparison tests which way of restoring dependence across substations gives the better summed ensemble. Ensemble copula coupling (ECC) orders each substation's calibrated quantiles by the ranks of the ensemble members. The Schaake shuffle orders them by a template taken from history. Both then give members whose sums across substations are scored against the observed sum, with the variogram score. It reuses the members of N8 (experiment 125) and takes about 1 CPU hour. It is the comparison that CAL's coherence pass needs, so CAL can reuse its result.

    Tail quantile regression on filtered residuals

    The proposal fits a quantile regression on the Kalman filter's residuals, with the model's prediction, its square, and the hour of day as covariates. de Vilmarest et al. (2024) did this for net load in the 14 GB grid supply point groups, and the regression gave a small gain over Gaussian quantiles on the same residuals. They also adapted the regression online by gradient descent on the pinball loss. In their results nearly all of the improvement came from filtering the mean, and the quantile step added 2 to 6%. Their data are regional and national load, so the gain at Primary substations is untested. The method replaces the residual-quantile spread of the convex model, and runs in CPU seconds.

    Conditional Pareto tails

    The proposal models the quantiles beyond 1% and 99% with a generalised Pareto distribution whose scale depends on covariates. Browell and Fasiolo (2021) found that plain quantile regression is not calibrated beyond those probabilities. A conditional Pareto tail cut the upward reserve requirement by up to 24.6% and the downward reserve by up to 10.8%, against a static Pareto tail. Their fit uses the R package mgcv, so a Python version needs a small custom maximum-likelihood fit, which takes CPU minutes per substation. The proposal serves the curtailment goal, which depends on the extreme upper quantiles.

    ERA5-only winter-by-winter backtest, earlier winters

    The backtest replaces the forecast weather with ERA5, so that the release candidate and XGBoost can be judged over the winters that the ensemble-weather experiments do not cover. Experiment 122 covers the winters of 2023-24 to 2025-26, so this backtest covers 2019-20 to 2022-23 and has low priority. The release candidate is the convex model with the Kalman filter (GAM-K), plus whichever of the night steps and the multi-hinge heating terms win their experiments. XGBoost is the production recipe (BG2) and its lagless version. ERA5 removes forecast-weather error, so the result ranks model structure on past weather and does not give production skill. The report gives winter-by-winter paired contrasts next to the ensemble-weather result, and says whether the two rankings agree. The run is cheap and uses the CPU only.
