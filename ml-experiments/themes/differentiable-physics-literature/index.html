    Differentiable physics: literature scan

    This page holds bare-bones notes from a small literature scan, made on 2026-10-20 to inform the design of the differentiable-physics experiments. A Sonnet agent read the papers listed in the last section in full. Every number below comes from the full text of the paper named, and all the papers are held in the project's private library folder literature/papers/. The notes are a working record, not a reviewed survey. The project's main review is The current state of the art in energy forecasting. The papers report a normalised root mean square error (nRMSE) and a normalised ranked probability score (nRPS), each in its own normalisation.

    Scope caveat for all GAM results: they are regional or national load (GB GSP Groups, France, US cities), day-ahead, not Primary substations.

    1. Tricks we're missing (ranked)

    1. Put the Kalman state on the coefficients of the frozen GAM effects, with learned diagonal noise, not only on a level plus anomaly.
      • Paper: de Vilmarest et al. (2024), Sec. II-B, Table I. Freeze the fitted effects, standardise them, and let the weight vector theta_t of (effects, intercept) follow a random walk. Q is diagonal, found by greedy likelihood (R package viking).
      • Gain (14 GB GSP Groups, 2021, nRMSE): Kalman-dynamic 0.332 vs offline GAM 0.635, GAM refitted yearly 0.387, GAM refitted daily 0.377, Kalman with Q=0 (static) 0.380. So: Q=0 only matches daily refits; the learned Q is what wins (about 12% better than daily refitting).
      • Second source: de Vilmarest and Goude (2022), Table III (MAE in MW, competition month): linear model offline 22.8, dynamic 14.9, dynamic with Q set big and proportional to I 11.2, VIKING 11.5. Q proportional to I was best there but not in the pre-competition data, so the Q choice depends on how much the data drifts.
      • Slot-in: after the convex fit, freeze the fitted columns (daily profile, heating, solar, holiday). Run a Kalman filter whose state is one multiplier per block of hours on each column. Experiment 106 filtered only level and anomaly; this lets heating sensitivity and PV scale adapt.
      • Cost: CPU seconds per site per year for the filter; minutes to fit Q on a training year.
    2. Let the Kalman state learn capacities, so the explicit capacity input matters less.
      • Paper: de Vilmarest et al. (2024), Sec. IV-D. Remove the embedded solar and wind capacity inputs: offline nRMSE rises by more than 10%; Kalman-adapted nRMSE falls by 0.4%. Scope: 2019, GB GSP Groups.
      • Slot-in: our convex PV capacity is already non-decreasing; a PV-scale multiplier in trick 1 gives a cheap second estimate, and the gap between the two flags capacity steps.
      • Cost: free once trick 1 exists.
    3. Fit the quantile regression on the Kalman residuals (not the offline residuals), with the GAM prediction, its square, and hour-of-day as covariates; optionally adapt it online.
      • Paper: de Vilmarest et al. (2024), Table II (nRPS, 2019 / 2020 / 2021): offline GAM + offline quantile regression 0.231 / 0.338 / 0.454; Kalman Gaussian quantiles 0.212 / 0.217 / 0.222; Kalman + offline quantile regression 0.206 / 0.214 / 0.217; Kalman + online gradient descent with step size chosen by Bernstein online aggregation (BOA) 0.202 / 0.201 / 0.209.
      • Reading: nearly all the gain comes from filtering the mean (0.231 to 0.206 in 2019). The extra from the online quantile step is 2 to 6%.
      • Slot-in: replace our residual-quantile spread by the covariate quantile regression. Online gradient descent on the pinball loss is five lines. Cost: CPU seconds.
    4. Covariate-dependent Generalised Pareto tails for the extreme quantiles.
      • Paper: Browell and Fasiolo (2021), Sec. V-B and Table IV. Quantile regression on GAM residuals for the body; GPD beyond the 1st and 99th percentile with a scale that depends on covariates. Plain quantile regression is not calibrated beyond 1% and 99%. The conditional GPD cut upward reserve by up to 24.6% and downward by up to 10.8% against a static GPD (probability levels 0.01% to 0.25%).
      • Fit in mgcv (R); in Python it needs a small custom maximum-likelihood fit. Fits our curtailment-tail goal. Cost: CPU minutes per site.
    5. Learn the observation variance online (VIKING) rather than a fixed sigma.
      • Paper: de Vilmarest (2022); used in de Vilmarest and Goude (2022) Table III (VIKING 11.5 vs dynamic 14.9 on the linear model; no better than hand-set big Q). Gain is modest and mainly saves tuning. Cost: CPU seconds.
    6. Exponentially smoothed temperatures with two time constants, plus smoothed daily min and max.
      • Obst et al. (2021), Sec. 3: GAM terms f(Temp95), f(Temp99), f(TempMin99, TempMax99), f(t, Temp), factors per step on half-hourly data. Also the GEFCom2014 winner's smoothed temperature, alpha = 0.95 (Fasiolo et al. (2021), Sec. 7). No paper isolates the gain.
      • Slot-in: add two decayed columns (our 6 h to 1 week means are the same idea) plus smoothed daily extremes. Cost: minutes.
    7. Detrend the target before the tree model.
      • Rubattu et al. (2023), Sec. 3: fit a linear trend in the training fold, subtract it, fit LightGBM, add it back. Shown in Fig. 3 as zero-mean residuals; no number. Same paper: temporal-hierarchy combination improved CRPS about 5% and interval score about 10% (BigDEAL 2022 data, not public). For the XGBoost side of the blend only.
    8. Intraday autoregressive correction of the residuals, one model per horizon.
      • de Vilmarest and Goude (2022), Sec. III-B and Table II: offline GAM MAE 20.7 to 19.3 MW in a stable period; MLP 57% lower in the unstable year. Likely already covered by our anomaly state; check before building.

    2. Our current choices against the literature

    • Fixed 15.5 C hinge: no paper found uses a hinge. All the GAM papers above fit a free smooth of temperature (cubic spline) and let the data find the threshold (Fasiolo et al. (2021) f(T) with rank 15; Obst et al. (2021)). So the literature neither supports nor contradicts a fixed threshold; a spline would be the check, and a learned threshold is the nearer middle road.
    • Smoothed temperatures (6 h, 1 d, 3 d, 1 week): supported. Every EDF-line paper uses exponential smoothing at two time constants (0.95 and 0.99 per step); see trick 6. With half-hourly steps those are roughly 10 h and 50 h (the scan's own conversion, not stated in the paper).
    • Monthly drift knots: weakly supported. The literature's answer to level shifts is the Kalman coefficient state, which beat yearly and daily refits (trick 1). The finding of experiment 106 (drift adds nothing once the filter runs) agrees. No paper tested monthly spline knots.
    • Kalman level and anomaly states: supported, but narrower than the literature's state (trick 1). Adapting only the level discards the slow-changing heating and PV sensitivities that de Vilmarest et al. found drifted.
    • Residual-quantile spread (per block of hours and quarter): supported as a first step (Table II: offline quantile regression on filtered residuals already gives most of the gain), but poorly calibrated in the tails (Browell and Fasiolo: beyond 1% and 99%).
    • Convex, non-negative, non-decreasing PV capacity: no paper found tests monotonic capacity. de Vilmarest et al. let the capacity coefficient move freely and it learned embedded capacity (trick 2). Tang et al. (2024) estimate capacity with a neural network from 24 h net-load curves, so a convex physical fit is a different route.

    3. Hierarchy

    • Reconciliation barely helps the bottom level. Nespoli et al. (Swiss low-voltage grid, 24 bottom series): minT with graphical-lasso covariance cut aggregate RMSE by up to 10% but bottom-level RMSE by under 1%; minT with Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage made the bottom level worse. Nespoli et al.'s suggested fix: reconcile conditionally on the previous steps' forecast errors.
    • Projection helps little when every node uses the same model. Brégère and Huard (2022) (GB household populations, 3-month test, errors summed over all nodes): projection alone cut error about 1% for GAM, 5% for random forest; adding an online expert-aggregation step cut it a further 10 to 15%. Read: the gain came from mixing in other nodes' forecasts as experts, not from the constraint.
    • Probabilistic reconciliation: Jeon et al. (2019), temporal hierarchy, two Crete wind farms: reconciling ranked (not stacked) samples improved CRPS; best method cut 24 h CRPS by 26.6% and 21.1% at the two farms. Temporal, not across sites, so only indirect support for our sums.
    • No paper found that tested ensemble copula coupling, the Schaake shuffle, or the variogram score on substation demand; none found for soft hierarchical losses or hierarchical Bayesian load models with measured bottom-level gains. Searches: arXiv API titles and our library. Not exhaustive.
    • Practical reading: for Primaries (bottom), expect under 1 to 5% from reconciliation. The finding of experiment 36 (child forecast error is the limit) agrees. The larger payoff the literature shows is for BSP and GSP aggregates, and from online expert mixing.

    4. Solar disaggregation

    • Identification by regression on an irradiance proxy plus a load proxy. Kara et al. (2018): net active power regressed on measured reactive power (load proxy) and a nearby irradiance reading, daytime only, with a correction for capacitor-bank switches (threshold 90 kvar per phase). Recovered a 7.5 MWp site with RMSE about 450 kW (about 6%); validated against the site's own meter. Our analogue: MVAr, where NGED telemetry has it.
    • Unsupervised, validated on metered PV. Sossan et al. (2019): PV modelled as capacity-weighted sum over 21 tilt/azimuth planes from transposed GHI; four estimators; best (frequency-band method) nRMSE 4.6 to 5.6% for 4 Swiss houses (35.3 kWp) with ground-truth PV meters. Also tested at 50% and 25% of capacity.
    • Neighbour controls: Cheung et al. (2023) use neighbouring customers without PV as the load model; MAE 28.37% below a prior disaggregation model (Ausgrid, 300 customers). Needs known no-PV customers; at Primary level the equivalent is nearby Primaries with little PV.
    • Capacity from net-load shape. Tang et al. (2024): a model trained on summer data only had 18.1% lower RMSE than an all-year model with a third of the training data; Monday to Thursday only gave 26.9% lower RMSE; error about 10% once more than 20% of households have PV. Supports fitting PV capacity on the summer or shoulder windows only, and averaging curves (doubling averaged curves cut RMSE 25.1%, 17.5 to 13.1 kW).
    • Validation pattern across the papers: a metered subset gives ground truth; sensitivity to sampling time and penetration; none validates at Primary scale against known capacity. A synthetic known-answer injection (as in the gate of experiment 109) is stricter than what they did.
    • Not read in full (so no claims): Kanchana et al. (2026) GAN data curation (paywalled page only).
