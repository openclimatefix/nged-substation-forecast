    Experiment 46: how much of the best guess's daylight error is embedded solar?

    We asked how much of the error in a 0-to-10-day forecast of a Primary substation's power comes from solar panels behind the substation's meter. A Primary substation is a 33/11 kV or 66/11 kV substation in the electricity distribution network. The meter reads demand minus the panels' output, so a sunnier day than forecast lowers the metered power. The demand forecast is the best-guess recipe of experiment 00033, which normally takes shortwave and longwave radiation inputs, and which we refitted without those inputs. We added a correction to that forecast: the panels' capacity, estimated in experiment 00040, times how much sunnier or cloudier the day is than the average for that month and half-hour. We built the correction twice. The first copy read the true sunshine, taken from the satellite-based CAMS data (the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service), which no real forecast has. The second copy read the sunshine that the ECMWF ensemble weather forecast (ENS) predicted. We scored both on 68,903 daylight half-hour forecasts per random seed (3 seeds), at 5 Primary substations, in April to September. The score is the quantile score, our probabilistic forecast error score: an estimate of the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), in points, where a point is 1% of the substation's capacity. Knowing the true sunshine lowered the quantile score by about 1 point, which is about 20% of the 4.72 points that the demand forecast without radiation inputs scores. The correction built from the weather forecast lowered it by 0.06 points. That is within the noise, and short of the 0.35 points that the maintainer judged worth the effort of adding the correction to the live forecast, a judgement about the cost of porting. The verdict is refuted.

    The verdict is refuted: the ENS solar term does not reach the 0.35-point threshold. The oracle shows that the missing gain lies in the sunshine forecast, not in the capacity. The rows are the 5 anonymised series (named D9, D10, D11, D14, and D16) whose capacity estimate is clean; anonymised series are labelled D followed by a number so that no metered generator or site can be identified.

    PVDIS-5 is the planned next experiment in the series. It tests the solar term again, with weekly capacity estimates in place of the 2023/24 estimate.

    The ENS solar term scores no better than the best guess, so PVDIS-5 is a non-inferiority and explanation test, not a gain test. C6 is not wholly below zero, which is what the decision rule needs for a gain test. C6 supports "no better than d0". It does not support "no worse than d0", because the plan set no non-inferiority margin and the pooled upper end is +0.141. C6 also changes sign. By valid month, April to September, it is -0.09, -0.12, +0.19, +0.23, +0.15, and -0.11. By series it is wholly above zero at D10 (+0.131, +0.027 to +0.233) and D9 (+0.264, +0.024 to +0.423), and wholly below zero at D16 (-0.248, -0.448 to -0.061). A non-inferiority test in PVDIS-5 therefore needs a margin set in advance. By lead band, C6 is +0.073 (-0.195 to +0.322) at 0 to 24 hours and -0.014 (-0.144 to +0.112) at 1 to 2 days, with 6 resampling blocks each. The other bands are +0.054 (-0.104 to +0.192) at 2 to 6 days, +0.049 (-0.099 to +0.147) at 6 to 10 days, and +0.049 (-0.028 to +0.124) from 10 days.

    The ENS forecast carries the oracle's gain only to about 2 days

    The ENS anomaly tracks the CAMS anomaly for about 2 days, then fades. C3 follows the same pattern. On the training window, the correlation of the ensemble-mean anomaly with the CAMS anomaly runs from 0.77 to 0.81 at 0 to 24 hours, 0.73 to 0.77 at 1 to 2 days, 0.56 to 0.60 at 2 to 6 days, 0.25 to 0.29 at 6 to 10 days, and 0.02 to 0.04 from 10 days, across the six series. The oracle gains at every lead (C4 from -0.59 to -1.21). The ENS version gains -0.262 (-0.598 to +0.037) at 0 to 24 hours and -0.277 (-0.406 to -0.144) at 1 to 2 days, then -0.079, -0.002, and +0.011 in the bands beyond. All of these intervals are at 95%, exploratory.

    Fragility of the small gains

    • Without D9, the series with the largest bound on its capacity, C3 is -0.113 (-0.230 to -0.000, rounded; the interval touches zero) at 97.5%. The rule would then give small gain, but the upper end sits at zero, so the reading is fragile and the pre-registered verdict, which includes D9, stands.
    • C3 is not the same sign in every valid month. It is negative in April (-0.204), May (-0.232), and September (-0.262), and positive in June (+0.117), July (+0.183), and August (+0.024). C4 is negative in all six months. The intervals resample 7 blocks, each a month of forecast start dates (March to September, because forecasts started in late March are valid in April).
    • C3 varies by series and sky. It is -0.382 (-0.588 to -0.182) at D16 and positive at D5 and D9 (+0.278 and +0.173). By sky it is -0.719 in cloudy rows, +0.225 (+0.076 to +0.329) in middle rows, and -0.135 in sunny rows, all exploratory at 95%.
    • A week-block bootstrap (29 blocks) gives the same answer: C3 -0.056 (-0.197 to +0.084) and C4 -0.966 (-1.248 to -0.663) at 97.5%.

    The fixed coefficient of 1 was too large

    The residual of d1l's median has a slope of -0.771 on the oracle shift. A forecast with no solar information would show -1. The slope is -0.626 at 0 to 24 hours, -0.632 at 1 to 2 days, -0.709 at 2 to 6 days, -0.818 at 6 to 10 days, and -0.848 from 10 days, and it holds on odd and even months alike (-0.780 and -0.762). By series it runs from -0.615 (D9) to -1.044 (D16). A stale capacity that is too small would push the slope past -1, not towards zero, so staleness does not explain the slope. About a quarter of the anomaly is probably already captured by the cloud proxies in d1l (dew point, precipitation, and the lagged power columns).

    A coefficient fitted on the training window, applied only in the three lead bands where the pooled slope was weaker than -0.8 in the log's own test (0 to 24 hours, 1 to 2 days, and 2 to 6 days), gives an exploratory C3 of -0.143 (-0.231 to -0.057) and a C4 of -1.057 (-1.401 to -0.784) at 95%. Even the fitted version lies well above -0.35.

    How the experiment departed from its plan, and what it could not show

    • Check 1 is relaxed from a 0.05-point match to 0.2 points, post hoc. The check compares the refitted d0 with experiment 00033's n1_refit. The 0.127 is the mean absolute gap between the per-row raw medians of two identical D10 d0 fits, in points of capacity. It is not a score difference. The 18 comparisons printed 0.020 to 0.185 points. Check 7 (all arms hold the same 909,414 keys) passed. The stored ENS anomaly equals the recomputed slope times the ensemble-mean anomaly on all 307,476 validation rows.
    • Fit noise at the score level is small next to every interval. We measured it from the spread across the 3 seeds with e00046_seed_noise.py (log: seed_noise.log). On the 5 verdict series, the d0 score has a standard deviation of 0.0018 to 0.0027 per series and 0.0004 pooled. The C6 comparison has 0.0017 to 0.0050 per series and 0.00015 pooled (per seed 0.04788, 0.04764, and 0.04762), and d0 minus d1l has 0.0007 pooled. D5, which is outside the verdict series, is noisier (0.0058 for d0, 0.0060 for C6). Fit noise is therefore about 1/670 to 1/20 of C6's half-width (0.100), so it cannot move C6 across zero. The bootstrap draws one seed per resample, so its interval already covers single-fit noise. An Opus review of this write-up checked this reading.
    • C3 and C4 carry no fit-to-fit noise within a seed, but C3 still varies across seeds. Both come from one prediction pass of one d1l booster, so the paired difference does not include the difference between two fits. C3 still varies across seeds (pooled standard deviation 0.0009, from the same script) because each seed's shift sits on a different booster. C6 and d0 minus d1l compare different fits and carry fit noise.
    • The ENS climatology rests on one summer of training data. The anomaly is each forecast minus the mean for that calendar month and half-hour in the same data product, so a summer that differs from the training summer shifts the whole anomaly. On the scored rows the mean CAMS anomaly is +0.0245 of unit output (output as a fraction of installed capacity), and the mean calibrated ENS anomaly is +0.0116, which as shifts are 0.884 and 0.416 points of capacity. By month, the mean CAMS shift runs from +2.877 points in April to -1.016 in July, and the mean ENS shift from 0.091 to 0.678.
    • The 2023/24 capacity is probably out of date, so the oracle is conservative.

    What the next experiment should answer

    The oracle shows that about 1 point of quantile score is available, and the ENS term collects only the part within 2 days. A next experiment could take one of three routes, and the project has not committed to any. The first is a lead-banded term, used only within 2 days, with a coefficient fitted on the training window per lead band, so the term fades where ENS has no skill. PVDIS-5 could then be a non-inferiority and explanation test overall, plus a gain test pre-registered for 0 to 48 hours only, with PVDIS-4's weekly capacity vintages in place of the 2023/24 capacity. The second is a better short-range solar forecast, because the irradiance forecast is the bottleneck and not the capacity: a satellite-based nowcast for 0 to 6 hours is one candidate, and a second weather forecast beside ENS is another. The third is to stop, since the gain is confined to short leads, where the lagged power columns of d1l probably already carry some of the signal.
