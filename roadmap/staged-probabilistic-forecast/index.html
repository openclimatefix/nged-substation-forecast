    A staged probabilistic forecast

    Status: 🔬 Research. The design on this page is a best guess under test. The ML experiments pages hold the experiments that test it, and each theme's Research plan page lists the experiments to run next. No production code implements it.

    The page describes a probabilistic power forecast in three parts with fixed roles. Stage 1 turns weather into power, stage 2 calibrates the result, uses lagged power, and blends weather products, and coupling restores coherent ensemble members. Each part always runs. The page states the aims, the design, the evidence so far, the alternatives, and the open questions.

    Terms used below. NWP means numerical weather prediction. ENS is the ECMWF (European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts) ensemble, with 51 members per run, served by Dynamical.org from 2024-04-01. UKV is the Met Office's deterministic regional model, and MOGREPS-UK is the Met Office's regional ensemble. ERA5 is ECMWF's reanalysis and CAMS is the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service's satellite-derived solar irradiance; the docs call these "estimates of past weather". A series is one half-hourly power time series (a substation, a solar farm, or a wind farm), and version 2 targets 2,500 of them. A quantile model is trained with pinball loss. PIT is the probability integral transform, a histogram that shows calibration. EMOS is ensemble model output statistics. ECC is ensemble copula coupling, which turns a calibrated marginal distribution back into ensemble members that keep their dependence across time and series, and ECC-Q does it by sorting quantiles against member ranks. Clear-sky power is the power a site would produce under a cloudless sky. ERA5T is the preliminary release of ERA5, which lands about 5 days late. GEFS is the Global Ensemble Forecast System of the United States' National Centers for Environmental Prediction. CEDA is the Centre for Environmental Data Analysis, which hosts a UKV archive. MIDAS is the Met Office Integrated Data Archive System. A GPU is a graphics processing unit.

    Aims

    The aim is not only a skilful forecast on day one. A design that wins today's leaderboard but cannot take a second weather model is the wrong design. The aims, in priority order:

    1. A skilful, calibrated probabilistic power forecast at 2,500 series, judged by the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) and the mean absolute error (MAE).
    2. Easy addition of new NWP products, including products with very short histories. Blending many NWP products is a strong hunch for improving the forecast. Some of the best products have too little history to train a full forecasting model on (MOGREPS-UK, a few months). Some are deterministic (UKV). Some have different step widths (hourly beyond day 5, against ENS's 3-hourly then 6-hourly) or different variable conventions (a mean over the previous period, an instantaneous value, or a change between the two inside one product's life).
    3. Reuse of the weather-to-power model ("stage 1") elsewhere in the system: switching-event detection, effective-capacity estimation, data cleaning (metering-fault detection), and the lag-anomaly features (how unusual was each lagged power value, given the weather then).
    4. Coherent ensemble members. The grid physics must be coherent within each ensemble member (a member that is very windy for one wind farm is windy for all of them). Coherence matters most beyond day 7, where ENS members disagree sharply about wind speed and irradiance.
    5. Explainability. An engineer should be able to say which stage produced an error.
    6. Inherent stability. Each stage always runs. No conditional skips a stage. A missing input is a null that XGBoost routes, never a raised exception.
    7. Research first. Experiments test every idea before production code changes.

    Aims 2 and 5 are the reason the forecast chain has more than one learned stage. Each stage has one job: stage 1 turns weather into power, stage 2 calibrates and blends the products, and the coupling step restores coherent members. One model doing several of those jobs is harder to explain, to test, and to extend with a new product. Aim 3 needs stage 1 only as an offline artefact that runs on analysis weather, so aim 3 does not by itself justify serving the chain. Stage 1 also runs at serving time on analysis weather, or on the analysis proxy, to compute the lag anomalies.

    Design principles traded away. The staged design departs from three of the design principles. Principle 2 (complexity belongs offline, not in the serving path) is met for training but not for serving, where the adapter, stage 1, stage 2, and coupling all run on every forecast. Principle 3 (one execution path from research to production) is strained because stage 1 has no lead time and so does not fit the BaseForecaster interface. Principle 6 (the whole system runs on one laptop) requires three chained trained artefacts (stage 1, the stage 2 calibrator, and the blend) to run on one machine. In return the design buys aims 2 and 5. The experiments keep the stage count to what they prove: a stage that does not earn its place is removed.

    The design

    The best-guess design has three parts with fixed roles: stage 1 turns weather into power, stage 2 calibrates, uses lagged power, and blends the products, and coupling restores coherent members. Every part always runs, and each has one job. The experiments so far support a stage 1 trained on analysis weather, and support lagged power in stage 2. They do not support stage 2 for a single product without lags, so stage 2 must earn its place under the two conditions it exists for: several weather products and lagged power. The main challenger is a single-stage quantile XGBoost model on ENS statistics with a small correction per additional product. A learned weather-correction stage ("stage 0") and a learned persistence stage ("stage 1b") are not in the first design; see the alternatives.

    The adapter handles known facts as code. Each NWP has a table of step width, accumulation or instantaneous convention, and the date each convention applies from. The adapter resamples every NWP (and ERA5 and CAMS) to the same half-hourly grid in the same convention. Two parts exist: short-wave radiation conserves each native window's mean and long-wave radiation uses a shape-preserving cubic interpolant (PR #1166 (Resample short-wave and long-wave radiation over native intervals)), and wind travels as eastward and northward components, with speed and bearing derived afterwards (PR #1167 (Retain wind components through interpolation)). Learning conventions from data would waste a short history and could not be unit-tested. A validation check (diurnal profile against solar geometry) catches an undeclared convention change and raises a WARN asset check, not an exception.

    Stage 1 is NWP-agnostic and lead-agnostic. The experiments call stage 1 "stage 1a" when it has no lags and no lead. It trains on analysis weather (ERA5 and CAMS) to power. Most trial-area power series go back to late 2019 and the ERA5 ingest starts in 2020, so it could use up to roughly 7 years against the roughly 2 years of ENS. Experiments 00001 and 00003 found that the extra years do not help generators and hurt demand, so the training start is a setting for each technology. Stage 1 outputs conditional quantiles of power given the weather, which cover what remains uncertain when the weather is known (cloud flicker, unmetered load, site variation). The forecast chain uses the per-member median as the member's point value and, as a baseline, the mixture of the quantiles over members. CAMS on disk covers the six solar farms and the 22 demand series of the experiments: hourly point downloads from 2019-09-01 in data/studies/downloads/reanalysis/CAMS, fetched for issue #784 (Throwaway experiment: does the beam/diffuse split help an XGBoost PV forecast?) and PR #1201 (Download and validate CAMS irradiance at the demand series' locations). Stage 1 for 2,500 series needs CAMS at every series' location, and the service is a point API (see the data-sources note on using the point API), so CAMS at every series is a fetch of its own. The experiments so far run stage 1 on the 31-series row set.

    Stage 1 is a perfect-prognosis model, and that is a risk. Trained on analysis weather, it learns the sharp relationship that satellite irradiance supports (CAMS 5.09% MAE against ERA5's 9.08% on the solar studies' row set), and analysis cloud carries about twice the gain forecast cloud does. It will lean on variables that are much less informative in a forecast. A variance-preserving stage 0 does not close that gap. The repo's explainer on why a power model trained on past weather does not hedge names the same trade-off as perfect prognosis against model output statistics, and cites Marzban, Sandgathe and Kalnay (2006) for model output statistics beating perfect prognosis on mean squared error. Experiment 00001 found a gain over an ENS-trained model up to day 6 and none beyond, which is consistent with that risk. The deciding contrast stays the full chain against a single-stage model trained directly on ENS, by lead band, and a stage 1 trained on ENS early leads remains an arm.

    Lagged power enters stage 2, as lagged power and as lag anomalies. A lag anomaly is the observed power at a lag time minus stage 1's expectation at that time, given the weather then. It tells stage 2 how far recent power sat from what the weather explains, which is the "delta" that stage 2 needs to read the lags. Experiment 00005 supports this placement over a separate persistence stage that predicts the residual from the lags, as the 00005 bullet under "What the experiments show" gives. The roadmap already has a related idea (weather-delta compensation for power lags). Three risks apply. The lag anomalies of 00005 are ceilings, because serving has ERA5T about 5 days late and CAMS about 1 day late. Flagged or stuck-meter lags enter as nulls. A lag no longer than the lead is nullified, so the lead decides which lags exist. Stage 1 alone stays lag-free and lead-free, and reusable for aim 3.

    Stage 2 is one calibrator shared by all products, followed by a learned blend. The calibrator is one function with identical feature names for every product: that product's mean, spread, and percentiles of stage 1 power across its members, the lead, and the product-independent lagged power and lag anomalies. It is fitted on the pooled rows of all products, per series (experiment 00005 fitted one per series), with a multiplicative spread factor per product, and it outputs a location plus a positive scale times a fixed quantile shape. The blend combines the calibrated distributions of the products that are present. A product that is absent gets no weight and the other weights renormalise, so the stage always runs and a missing product is a degradation, not an exception. A deterministic product has zero spread, so its scale term needs a lead-dependent intercept. A product with a short horizon fades its weight to zero towards its last lead. ENS alone is the one-component case. Stage 1's values on the ENS-era months are forward-chained out-of-fold, or stage 2 would see stage 1 predictions that are too good. Experiment 00005 trained the calibrator as one multi-quantile XGBoost model per series at 9 levels, on every third daily ENS run.

    A new product joins stage 2 as extra rows in the pooled fit plus a spread factor and a blend weight, not as new columns. An XGBoost model given an NWP identifier splits on it freely, so the identifier cannot act as a scale adjustment only. Separate feature columns per product also do not transfer what the model learned: splits learned on the ENS spread column do not apply to a UKV spread column. Null versus non-null also acts as a date flag, because the new product's columns are non-null only in the most recent months, so the trees can split on "recent month" and absorb any drift in that period. A 3-month overlap is millions of rows but only about 30 to 45 independent weather episodes (the estimate in the training-history roadmap). The experiments keep one model with per-product columns as a control arm (the "per-product-columns control"), which a new product retrains in full. The shared calibrator above is the alternative the experiments test against it.

    Challenger: a single-stage ENS champion with a per-product correction. The champion is a quantile XGBoost model trained on ENS only, with stage 1 statistics, lead, lagged power, and lag anomalies as inputs. Each additional product adds a correction and the champion is never retrained. The correction shifts the champion's location by a × Δ and its log-scale by b × |Δ| + c × (the product's spread), where Δ is the product's stage 1 median minus the ENS stage 1 median, and a, b, and c vary smoothly with lead, are pooled per technology, and shrink towards zero. A missing product gives zero correction, so a product enters with zero correction by construction, and the safe-entry check of the second-product experiment tests that claim. The correction cannot learn interactions between a product and the lags, which is probably a small loss (the lead column gained only 0.05 points in quick-wins) but is unmeasured. The long ENS record supports this design for generators, although the project's own demand results (00003, 00006) found that a long history hurts demand: in the study of Lang et al. (2020), on 2 m temperature at central-European stations, training on several years beat a sliding window of recent days, including across an ECMWF model change. This design is also the baseline that stage 2 must beat or tie before stage 2 earns its place.

    The blend is learned, with few parameters, because a fixed blend is not calibrated by construction. An equal-weight mixture of individually calibrated distributions is generally over-dispersed (Ranjan and Gneiting (2010) show that a weighted average of distinct calibrated forecasts is uncalibrated, in a case study of binary probability-of-precipitation forecasts), and averaging quantiles re-creates under-dispersion (see the explainer). The beta-transformed linear pool fixes this with two shape parameters and nests the plain linear pool (when both equal 1); Gneiting and Ranjan (2013) extend it to full predictive distributions. We have read abstracts and secondary summaries of both papers, not the full texts. Stage 2b has two learned parts, fitted on held-out months: one weight per product, smooth in lead and shrunk towards equal weights when the overlap is short, and the two shape parameters, smooth in lead. A mixture already widens when the product medians disagree, so a separate disagreement input is added only if an experiment shows the beta-transformed pool is still under-dispersed when products disagree. The control arm is a fixed equal-weight mixture. The studies score both with the PIT histogram and coverage by lead band.

    Width must be monotone in spread, and XGBoost does not guarantee it. XGBoost monotone constraints bound a prediction against a feature, not the difference between two quantiles (both bounds can rise while the gap shrinks). The experiments therefore parameterise the calibrated distribution as a location plus a positive scale times a fixed quantile shape, with the scale monotone in spread, and test the width property directly on held-out data.

    Pooling stage 1 quantiles over members is a valid baseline, not double counting. If stage 1 estimates the distribution of power given the weather and the members are draws of the weather, the mixture over members follows from the law of total probability. The quick-wins study's 57% coverage of a nominal 80% band is not explained, and experiment 00006 used out-of-fold stage 1 on analysis weather and still covered only 83% to 85% of a nominal 90% band. Stage 2 must beat the mixture baseline (with and without a simple spread rescaling) to earn its place. The repo's double-counting caveat applies to models trained on forecast weather, whose learned spread already includes some forecast error. Stage 1 trains on analysis weather, so its spread does not. The mixture itself is the pooling recipe in that explainer.

    One coherent distribution comes out of stage 2 and ECC. The marginal distribution per series and valid time is stage 2's quantiles. ECC-Q needs one quantile per member, so the coupling step interpolates the 19 stage 2 quantile levels to the levels i/52 for i = 1 to 51, with a stated tail rule beyond the outermost levels, and assigns them to members by the rank of each member's stage 1 median. Ties at night-time solar, where every member is zero, need no rule because every assigned quantile is also zero. Other ties (for example when weather is missing and the medians coincide) would, if broken by member identifier, give perfect rank correlation across the affected series and times and distort aggregate uncertainty, so they break by a dependence template instead: the ranks of the members' stage 1 medians at neighbouring times, or a template taken from history (the Schaake shuffle). The experiments test the template with the coherence scores. At short leads members are nearly identical and ranks are mostly noise, so the experiments score coupling by lead band with the variogram score and include the Schaake shuffle there. The output is always the 51 ENS members. Other NWPs enter stage 2 as pooled rows and a blend component and do not add members, which removes the question of how to pool members of different NWPs.

    Models are specific to each site for substations and pooled for solar and wind, as a decision of the maintainer. For Primary substations, experiment 00006 found one model per substation slightly better than one model per type with a series identifier (7.11 against 7.34 points of capacity), by a small unpaired margin whose intervals overlap. For bulk supply points and grid supply points the pooled model was marginally lower (8.93 against 9.06, and 23.91 against 23.97), and 00006 says the margin is not established. The quick-wins study found the pooled model with a site identifier its best arm. The evidence is mixed, so a later experiment may revisit the choice. Solar and wind farms share a model, because their output follows the weather and a farm has too little history to carry a model of its own. The pooled choice for solar and wind has no experiment behind it.

    Targets for solar and wind power. Stage 1 could predict a ratio for solar: power divided by clear-sky PV power, multiplied back by the known half-hourly clear-sky curve, so the output stays in megawatts (roadmap section). Quantile loss in ratio space needs weights equal to the clear-sky power so that it equals the megawatt loss. Rows with zero clear-sky power are dropped. The per-site clear-sky scale is unknown for embedded PV, so it comes from a trailing (not centred) rolling quantile of clear-sky-normalised output, or from a physics model for solar farms. For wind, the analogue is a base_margin from a power curve of forecast wind speed, with stage 1 learning the residual. The ratio target is tested once, as a stage 1 arm. Multiplying quantiles by a positive clear-sky value preserves their order, so the coupling step is unaffected.

    Lead time. Stage 0 (if built) sees all leads and takes the lead as an input. Stage 1 takes no lead. Stage 2 takes the lead, and the lead also decides which lag features exist: a lag shorter than or equal to the lead is nullified. Lead buckets (for example day 0 to 1, 1 to 6, 6 to 15) are one arm in a later experiment. In quick-wins the lead column gained 0.05 points, so a bucketed design must clear a low bar. The risks are thin data per bucket and quantile jumps at bucket boundaries.

    Training scenarios and cadence.

    • From scratch: take the cleaned power history from clean_nged_power_data. Fit stage 1 on ERA5 and CAMS, from the training start chosen for the technology, with forward-chained out-of-fold predictions: each block is predicted by a model trained only on earlier blocks, with a purge gap of at least one week. Run the adapter then stage 1 per ENS member over the NWP era, using the out-of-fold stage 1. Fit stage 2 on the per-product statistics. Score end-to-end CRPS on held-out months.
    • Monthly retrain: keep stage 1 frozen, because its predictions for the new month are out of sample. Choose between the candidate and the incumbent stage 2 by rolling-origin evaluation: fit each on months up to a cut-off and score both on the following months, which neither saw. Only after the promotion decision, refit the winner on all months. Scoring the newest month after fitting on it would favour the candidate. Stage 1 retrains yearly, or when cleaning rules change or sites are added.
    • A new NWP with short history: add an adapter table. Stage 1 is untouched. Stage 2's calibrator keeps its form: the new product's rows join the pooled fit with one new spread factor, and the blend gains the product's weight, shrunk towards equal weights. The second-product experiments on the weather products Research plan measure how few months of history this needs. The single-stage challenger instead adds a correction (a few numbers per technology) to a frozen champion, and the per-product-columns control retrains in full.

    Leakage rules. Stage 1 must not train on months that stage 2 or the end-to-end score uses for validation, and ERA5 overlaps the ENS era. Forward-chained out-of-fold predictions replace a two-sided cross-fit, because a two-sided fit would let stage 1 see later capacity, later embedded PV, and later cleaning outcomes, giving residuals smaller than those it meets when serving. Lag anomalies are built from the source and latency serving will have: ERA5T lands about 5 days late and CAMS about 1 day late, so short lags use the analysis proxy (the early leads of the latest ENS run), whose errors exceed ERA5's. Experiment 00005 did not do this, so its lag results are ceilings.

    Regime changes in the long history, including the COVID lockdowns. Stage 1 could train on power from late 2019 to 2026, which includes the COVID lockdowns (about 2020-03 to 2021-07, roughly 1.3 of about 7 winters). The lockdowns distort the occupancy and calendar response of demand far more than the weather-to-power response of wind and solar farms, so the question applies to demand series and net-load substations, not to generation. The docs already contain ideas for how to represent the lockdowns and for which datasets to ingest, but nothing is decided and nothing is ingested. The era covariates section of the training-history roadmap lists the candidate ways to represent the lockdown: a scalar in [0, 1] sourced from the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker (a daily UK stringency index) or from Google's COVID-19 Community Mobility Reports (closer to the causal driver, thinly supported by one national preprint); recency sample weights; and excluding the period. Both candidate series ended in 2022, so whether they are still downloadable is unchecked, and nothing has been downloaded. The section also records three cautions: the evidence is national or building-level and none of it is a distribution substation; a scalar that returns to 0 cannot say that the post-lockdown world differs from the pre-lockdown world; and the scalar breaks under a global model unless a customer-mix covariate exists. The design consequences are:

    • Stage 2 is unaffected by construction. It trains on the ENS era (2024-04 onward), which has no lockdown.
    • Stage 1 is where the choice lives. For a technology whose training starts in 2019, the forward-chained out-of-fold run predicts the 2020-21 blocks with models that never saw a lockdown, so those blocks carry large residuals. For demand, where the experiments point to a start in 2023-09, the lockdown lies outside the window, and the first forward-chained blocks train on fewer than 12 months, so a block starts only when a full year of training data exists. Those residuals matter only for the reuse cases (the roadmap already names the lockdown as the largest phantom event for the switching baseline, and as a free labelled test case) and not for stage 2.
    • The cheap arms have run for Primary substations (experiment 00006). A 0/1 indicator for 2020-03-23 to 2021-07-19, set to 0 in all forecast data, lowered the selection-fold error by 0.26 points, and it helped grid supply points most. Recency weights helped more, and a 2023-09 training start helped most on the confirmation year only. No Oxford or Google series has been downloaded, and the results give no reason to download one yet.

    Alternatives considered.

    • A learned weather-correction stage ("stage 0") that maps each NWP towards ERA5 and CAMS, member by member, before stage 1. It is a further learned artefact, a variance-preserving correction cannot close the perfect-prognosis gap, and a climatological quantile map has known defects (it does not fade with skill, it injects incoherent variance from coarse steps, and it breaks consistency between variables). Built only if the second-NWP experiment shows stage 2 failing. If built: fit at the NWP's own step width, correct wind speed and clear-sky index and not their components, smooth over lead, exclude the validation months, and include an identity arm.
    • Merge stages 0 and 1 (pretrain on ERA5 and CAMS, fine-tune per NWP with power): needs power data per NWP, which is the short-history problem, and suits a neural network better than XGBoost (an XGBoost warm start can only add trees). Studied only if stage 2 fails on a second NWP.
    • Pool the products as exchangeable members of one ensemble: a deterministic UKV would get a weight of 1/52. Hagedorn et al. (2012) found that a nine-system multi-model ensemble did not beat the ECMWF ensemble alone on extratropical temperature, and that calibrated ECMWF often matched the multi-model ensemble. The gain from blending products may therefore be smaller than hoped for solar, while the project's matched-lead study found a wind gain of 0.184 points of capacity.
    • Weather-space correction fitted on power error through the frozen stage 1, as the training-history roadmap proposes, instead of towards ERA5 and CAMS. The correction keeps each member's rank. The weather-space correction is an arm if stage 0 is built.
    • A persistence stage ("stage 1b") that predicts the residual of power against stage 1 from the residuals at the forecast origin and earlier, trained on ERA5 and CAMS with simulated origins and leads. Experiment 00005 found that lagged power in stage 2 beat it by 0.84 points, and that adding it on top of stage 2 gained 0.06 (both ceiling results). Revisit only if serving-latency lag anomalies weaken stage 2.
    • The single-stage ENS champion with a per-product correction: the challenger above. It drops a stage and is the baseline that stage 2 must beat or tie.
    • A physics stage 1 (differentiable physics, version 2): drops into the same slot with the same interface and has few parameters, so it suits new sites. Not studied now.
    • An NWP better than ERA5 (UKV at short leads, for local wind and cloud): the canonical past-weather estimate becomes a per-variable choice or blend, settled by the ceiling test.
    • Station observations (MIDAS Open): an offline referee and a candidate component of the past-weather blend. No live feed exists.
    • Direct CRPS training: a guess, not a sourced figure, puts its gain below 0.2 points over pinball loss at many levels. Runs only if the PIT histogram stays U-shaped.

    Interaction with switching detection, effective capacity, and metering faults. All three want "expected power from analysis weather and calendar", and all three share one risk, a loop between detection and training. The guard is the same each time: a robust fit, out-of-fold expectations, one versioned stage 1 artefact, and a rule-based detector first.

    • Switching-event detection: the roadmap's approach 1, the two-stage forecaster, is nearly this design. Stage 1 is the baseline and its residual is the switching signal. The residuals feed stage 2 as lag-anomaly features (the existing idea of residual lag features from the switching-detector baseline), and a recent detected event could widen stage 2's interval. Live detection runs on the analysis proxy.
    • Effective capacity: the PV ratio target needs a per-site clear-sky scale, which is an effective-capacity estimate, so capacity estimation feeds stage 1 and stage 1 feeds the cheap capacity baselines. The scale must be causal. A physics stage 1 would make capacity a fitted parameter.
    • Metering faults: large residuals against stage 1 could flag stuck meters and spikes, as a layer after the existing rule-based cleaning. Stage 1 would otherwise learn the faults it should detect, and multi-month faults span the blocks, so cleaning recall is tested on the known faults from PR #1159 before any reuse.

    What the experiments show

    Seven experiments and one closed study give the evidence so far. The ML experiments pages hold each experiment's question, verdict, and figures; the bullets give the headline of each. Pull request numbers for 00005 and 00006 are PR #1195 (Experiment 5: where lagged power enters a two-stage demand forecast) and PR #1202 (Experiment 6: which input features work best for the ERA5-trained demand model).

    • 00001, solar and wind, ENS only: stage 1 trained on ERA5 and applied to the 51 ENS members and averaged had a lower mean absolute error than the same columns trained on ENS weather, by 0.57 points of capacity for solar (interval −0.83 to −0.32) and 1.70 for wind (−2.33 to −1.08), up to day 6 and not beyond. The gain follows the training weather and not the training length.
    • 00002, solar and wind, ENS only, quantiles: pooling the ERA5-trained quantiles over the members beat an ENS-trained quantile model (CRPS −0.62 for wind, −0.24 for solar). A stage 2 calibrator with a learned blend, given one product and no lags, did not improve on the pooled mixture (wind +0.26) and was materially worse for solar (+0.40, interval +0.26 to +0.61); the learned blend changed CRPS by +0.00 to +0.08.
    • 00003, 22 series that are not wind or solar farms (16 demand series, 4 grid flows, and 2 others): with weather only, the ERA5-trained model was worse than the ENS-trained model by 1.37 points (+0.68 to +2.17), and with calendar columns and lags the two were equal (+0.10, −0.26 to +0.50). The error fell steadily as the training start moved later, which points to drift, with the customer mix, embedded generation, network changes, and the pandemic as candidate causes.
    • 00005, 16 Primary substations, ceiling arms (verdict: refuted, because the experiment predicted that a separate persistence stage would win): lagged power in the stage 2 calibrator beat a lag-free calibrated stage 1 by 1.80 points of quantile score (12 of 12 months, 16 of 16 series), and beat lagged power through a separate persistence stage ("stage 1b") plus a calibrator by 0.84 (99% interval +0.28 to +1.50). Lagged power through the persistence stage was the better placement in 8 of the 16 series, so the 0.84 is an average over mixed series. The experiment assumed no telemetry delay at the forecast origin, which is a second ceiling. Adding the persistence stage on top of lags in stage 2 gained 0.06 (−0.15 to +0.03). The advantage of stage 2 persisted at 3 to 15 days, and 9 of 12 months favoured lags in stage 2.
    • 00006, Primary substations, confirmation year: hour and weekday were the largest non-lag input (−0.46, 99.4% interval −0.61 to −0.30). A COVID indicator, a 14-day Christmas window, and recency weights helped, and a 2023-09 training start helped most (confirmation-year evidence only). Time of year added nothing, because the weather block already carries the season, and trailing temperature means and windchill showed no detectable gain.
    • 00007, Primary substations: a static correction of the stage 1 daytime residual with CAMS irradiance was inconclusive. CAMS added to ERA5 shortwave had a partial R² of 0.110, and the result rests largely on one substation, so it does not separate an irradiance error from a change in embedded solar capacity.
    • 00008, Primary substations: summaries of recent power (the minimum, mean, and maximum of the latest 24 hours and of the latest day on the same weekday) in the stage 2 calibrator added -0.02 points to raw lags up to 48 hours (98.75% interval -0.14 to +0.13). The interval is too wide to resolve 0.15 points, so the verdict is inconclusive.
    • Indicative inputs from a closed study (PR #1175 (Study XGBoost pipeline fixes, lean inputs and calibration diagnostics)): the radiation correction lowers solar pinball loss by about 0.34 points, and the pooled quantile forecasts under-cover for wind. No human reviewed those numbers.

    All lagged arms of 00005 are ceilings: the lag anomalies use ERA5 weather at past times, which serving does not have. The ceiling is the main open risk of the lag design, and the next experiments build the lag anomalies from the sources serving will have.

    Cost at version 2 scale

    Basis. The quick-wins study measured, for 31 series on one GPU, 37 s (squared error) and 5 min (9 quantile levels) for one model per series, 9.76 million training rows (production trains on the control member, about 315,000 rows per series), 8 GB peak memory, and a feature frame of 5 min 19 s and 5.3 GB, extrapolating to about 7 hours and 430 GB for 2,500 series in one process. That is about 9.7 s per series at 9 levels. The rates below assume cost grows linearly with series, rows, and quantile levels. The last assumption is unverified. Read everything as an order of magnitude.

    Stage Training at 2,500 series Inference per daily run
    Adapter Not trained Seconds to minutes, dominated by reading the NWP
    Stage 0 (if built) Not yet sized; depends on the form chosen About 1,000 cells (unverified) × 51 members × 720 half-hours × about 10 variables, about 370 million values
    Stage 1 About 105,000 rows per series at 9 levels: about 3 s per series, so about 2 GPU-hours per fit; about 6 fits for the forward-chained run plus the final fit, so about 12 GPU-hours, yearly About 92 million rows (51 members × 2,500 series × 720 half-hours): minutes on GPU, tens of minutes on 8 CPU cores
    Stage 2 One row per (init time, valid time), the production layout, with 2 to 3 years of ENS: about 15 to 25 s per series at 9 levels, so about 10 to 17 GPU-hours monthly if fitted per series; a per-type model would be cheaper but does not fit GPU memory at 2,500 series About 1.8 million rows: seconds

    Stage 2 is the stage whose cost scales worst. A monthly refit of 10 to 17 GPU-hours is a design constraint. Options for the experiments: fit stage 2 per series type with row subsampling, or on fewer lead steps. Each NWP added adds rows to the pooled fit and raises the cost. The feature frame and input/output still probably dominate wall-clock time and need chunking by series.

    Open questions

    • Which uses need coherence across time, across series, or both, and does feeder-to-substation hierarchy data exist for scoring it?
    • Is the target overall CRPS or exceedance at the 95th percentile? The answer decides between the location-and-scale form and a free quantile calibrator, which the calibration Research plan tests.
    • The final 51-member output fits PowerForecast, whose ensemble_member already has a different meaning for each forecaster (for climatology it is the rank of a quantile level). Should a docstring line say what the index means for the staged forecaster? The 19-level quantile table and the stage 1 tables need their own schema in the experiment store, and the contract is not changed without asking.
    • Stage 1 for the demand series now has ERA5 and CAMS at their locations. For the 2,500-series scale, is CAMS fetched at every series' location, given that the experiments found CAMS explains more of a Primary substation's residual than ERA5 shortwave (a post hoc result)?
    • The closed study of pipeline fixes found that pooled wind quantiles under-cover, and no merged experiment has measured wind coverage. Should a wind-specific calibration experiment come ahead of the multi-product work?
    • Which second NWP is tested first? The experiments can use a product already on disk, such as UKV from CEDA or a product downloaded from Open-Meteo, before any ingest into the production pipeline. The CEDA UKV archive differs statistically from the live feed, so a result on it may not carry over. Is a long-history ensemble such as GEFS obtainable?

    Work still to do

    Each theme's Research plan page lists the experiments to run next, and two pieces of infrastructure are tracked as GitHub issues because they change production-facing code. The Research plan pages are linked from the ML experiments overview. The two pieces of infrastructure are:

    • The conventions adapter. The radiation and wind rules have shipped. Remaining: step widths for hourly-stepped NWPs, the same adapter for ERA5 and CAMS, the per-NWP convention table, and the validation check, all with unit tests on synthetic input.
    • The scoring harness and coverage test. One scorer for every output: pinball loss at 19 levels from 0.05 to 0.95 plus a stated treatment of the tails beyond them, and the same estimator for ECC-coupled members (the fair-CRPS factor, which depends on the member count, can flip a comparison). PIT; coverage by lead band and series type; a synthetic spread-factor positive control; the in-sample against out-of-sample coverage test on the saved e1_quantile samples; one coherence score (CRPS of summed solar farms in a weather cell), with the energy and variogram scores added once coupled members exist.

    The ingest of a second NWP into the production pipeline also needs an issue, but only after the experiments on products already on disk show which product to ingest.
